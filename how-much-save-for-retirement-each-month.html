How much should you save for retirement each month? Work backward from the gap

A salary percentage can be a useful habit, but the contribution your plan needs comes from the retirement gap, time remaining and the assumptions behind it.

By CarrowmontPublished 18 November 2026 · Reviewed 18 November 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy
Want to calculate your own contribution?Open the Retirement Planner→

Use your current age, retirement age, savings, spending and assumptions to estimate the contribution path instead of relying on a generic percentage.

30-second answer

There is no universal monthly retirement amount. Work backward from the spending you want retirement assets to support, subtract reliable pension or other income, estimate the portfolio required, subtract the future value of savings you already have, then solve for the monthly contribution over the years remaining. Stress test the result because returns and inflation are uncertain.

Mandatory pension systems, employer contributions, tax relief and retirement-account limits differ by country. Treat the personal contribution as one part of total retirement funding, not as a universal product rule.

Why “save 10%” or “save 15%” is not a complete answer

A percentage of salary can create a useful automatic habit, but two people earning the same amount may need very different retirement contributions. One may already have a large pension balance and 30 years remaining. The other may have little saved, ten years remaining and a higher retirement spending goal.

The OECD’s 2026 pensions work emphasizes that contribution gaps differ across countries and population groups and that affordability matters. Someone carrying high-interest debt or lacking precautionary savings may need a different sequence than someone with stable cash flow and a generous employer pension.

Build the contribution from five inputs

  1. Retirement spending: Estimate what the household expects to spend, preferably separating essential from flexible costs.
  2. Reliable retirement income: Include pensions, annuities or other income only when the amount and timing are reasonably supported.
  3. Current retirement assets: Count money actually earmarked for retirement.
  4. Time remaining: The number of months until contributions stop is one of the strongest inputs.
  5. Return and inflation assumptions: Use them transparently and stress test lower-return or higher-inflation cases.

Step 1: Estimate the portfolio-funded spending gap

Annual portfolio-funded spendingExpected retirement spending − Reliable non-portfolio income

If retirement spending is 60,000 a year and reliable pension income is 25,000, the portfolio initially needs to support about 35,000 before tax differences and timing details. That does not mean 35,000 stays constant forever; inflation and changing expenses matter.

Step 2: Convert that spending gap into a target range

Withdrawal rules such as 4% or 25× spending can provide a first-pass reference, but they are not guarantees. The required portfolio depends on retirement length, asset allocation, fees, taxes, inflation and return sequence. Use a stress-tested target range rather than treating one multiple as a promise.

Step 3: Let existing savings do their share of the work

Current retirement assets can compound before retirement. The future value of those assets reduces the amount that future monthly contributions must build. The same principle means starting earlier can reduce the monthly burden even when total lifetime contributions are substantial.

Worked example: Solve for the monthly gap

Assume an illustrative retirement target of 1,000,000 in 20 years, current retirement savings of 200,000 and a smooth 5% annual return compounded monthly. Ignore tax, fees and contribution increases.

The existing 200,000 would grow to about 542,528 under that smooth assumption. The remaining target can then be funded by monthly contributions. The modeled contribution is about 1,113 per month.

This sensitivity is exactly why a high return assumption should not be used simply to make a contribution affordable. Real returns are uneven, fees and taxes reduce outcomes, and a 7% assumption can fail to arrive over the horizon that matters.

Contribution increases can share the burden with future income

If income is likely to rise, a plan can start with a manageable amount and increase the contribution each year. A 5% annual contribution step-up does not guarantee success, but it can reduce the pressure on today’s cash flow while still creating a path toward a higher future saving rate.

The risk is behavioural: A step-up only works if future increases actually happen. A plan that requires ten perfect annual increases is less robust than one that works with conservative assumptions and treats increases as an improvement.

Employer contributions belong in the math

Where an employer contributes to a pension or retirement account, include that funding rather than pretending you save alone. But understand vesting, eligibility, contribution limits and what happens if you change jobs. A match can make the first portion of employee contributions unusually valuable, but local plan rules control the result.

Do not sacrifice financial resilience to hit one retirement percentage

A retirement plan can be undermined if an emergency forces high-interest borrowing. Likewise, directing every spare unit to retirement while a very expensive card balance compounds can be inefficient. Build the contribution alongside cash flow, emergency savings and debt priorities.

That does not mean retirement can always wait. Delaying contributions for many years makes the later monthly requirement much larger. The goal is a sequence that is sustainable, not a false choice between “retirement” and “everything else.”

Use three contribution levels

LevelPurpose
FloorThe amount you can maintain even in a tight month, including any contribution needed to capture valuable employer benefits
TargetThe modeled contribution that keeps the base retirement scenario on track
StretchExtra saving used after bonuses, debt payoff or income increases to create margin

This makes the plan easier to follow than treating one exact number as success or failure.

Monthly retirement saving checklist

  1. Estimate retirement spending in today’s money.
  2. List pensions and other reliable income with start dates.
  3. Set a retirement age and stress test an earlier or later date.
  4. Record current retirement assets.
  5. Choose a conservative base return and inflation assumption.
  6. Calculate the required monthly contribution.
  7. Test a lower-return and higher-inflation scenario.
  8. Include employer contributions and contribution step-ups only when they are realistic.
  9. Review the result against emergency savings, debt and current cash flow.
  10. Recalculate after major salary, family, market or retirement-age changes.

Common mistakes

  • Using one salary percentage for every age and starting balance.
  • Ignoring public or employer pension income.
  • Using an optimistic return assumption to force the answer lower.
  • Forgetting fees, tax and inflation.
  • Counting a house or business as retirement capital without a plan to turn it into spendable cash.
  • Stopping contributions after one difficult year and never recalculating.

Frequently asked questions

Is 15% of income enough for retirement?

It may be enough for one plan and insufficient or excessive for another. The answer depends on starting age, current assets, retirement age, pension income, spending and returns. Use the percentage as a habit benchmark, not a personalized calculation.

Should I count employer contributions?

Yes, if they are expected to vest and remain part of your retirement assets. Keep employee and employer amounts visible so you understand how much comes from each source.

What if the calculated monthly amount is unaffordable?

Do not hide the gap with a higher return assumption. Test a later retirement date, lower spending target, higher future contribution, contribution step-up, additional income or a combination.

How often should I recalculate?

At least after major changes in income, retirement age, spending goals, pension rules or portfolio value. An annual review is a useful habit even when nothing dramatic changes.
