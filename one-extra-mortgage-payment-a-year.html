One extra mortgage payment a year: How much time and interest could it save?

A thirteenth-payment equivalent can materially change a long loan, but the result depends on rate, term, timing and lender rules.

By CarrowmontUpdated 21 Oct 2026Educational guideMethodology

Country-aware terminology

Carrowmont keeps the maths universal and adapts familiar mortgage or home-loan wording to the country you select. Lender rules and fees still depend on the actual loan contract.

Direct answer

One extra scheduled payment each year can shorten a long mortgage or home-loan term by several years, but there is no universal “five years saved” rule. The result depends on the interest rate, remaining term, when the extra payment is made, whether it is applied to principal, and any lender charges or restrictions.

Why one extra payment can have a much bigger effect than it looks

A principal-and-interest loan charges future interest on the balance that remains outstanding. When an extra amount is applied to principal, the balance becomes smaller earlier. Every later interest calculation then starts from that lower balance. The saving therefore comes from a chain reaction: the extra payment reduces principal, the lower balance reduces future interest, and more of later scheduled payments can then go toward principal.

The effect is strongest when the loan still has many years remaining. It is also larger at higher rates because each unit of balance removed would otherwise have generated more future interest. This is why the same extra-payment habit can save a different number of months on two otherwise similar loans.

Worked example: One extra payment at the end of each year

To keep the comparison clean, Carrowmont starts with a balance of 1000000, a 25-year remaining term, monthly scheduled repayments and a fixed rate. The extra amount equals one normal scheduled monthly payment and is applied at month 12, 24, 36 and so on until the loan is cleared. No fees, taxes, redraw, offset account or rate changes are included.

The timing of the extra payment matters

An extra payment made in January generally reduces principal for longer than the same amount paid in December. If cash is available throughout the year, spreading it across monthly extras can therefore produce a slightly different result from waiting to make one year-end lump sum. If the lump sum is only available at year-end, however, there is no reason to pretend it could have been paid earlier. The right comparison is the one that matches the real cash-flow pattern.

“One extra payment” must mean extra principal, not just paying early

Some payment arrangements change the calendar without necessarily increasing the amount applied to principal over the year. The important question is how much cash actually reduces the loan balance and when. A genuine thirteenth-payment equivalent increases annual principal repayment. Simply moving the same twelve payments to different dates may change interest timing slightly, but it is not the same thing as contributing a full extra payment.

What changes the result most?

  • Remaining term: extra principal usually has more time to work when many years remain.
  • Interest rate: a higher rate makes each avoided unit of balance more expensive, increasing potential interest savings.
  • Payment timing: earlier principal reduction generally saves more than the same amount paid later, all else equal.
  • Loan convention: daily interest, compounding conventions and lender rounding can produce a different payoff date from a simplified model.
  • Charges and limits: a prepayment penalty, early-repayment charge or contractual limit can reduce or delay the benefit.

Checklist: Make sure the payment is genuinely extra principal

  • Confirm that the normal scheduled payment remains due and the extra amount is not simply bringing a future installment forward.
  • Ask how to mark or instruct the lender to apply the extra amount to principal where that distinction exists.
  • Check annual overpayment limits, early-repayment charges and any processing fee before copying a generic “13th payment” strategy.
  • Compare one annual payment with the same annual cash spread monthly if both are realistic for your household.
  • Keep enough cash outside the mortgage for emergencies and near-term property costs.

Lender rules are local even when the maths is universal

The mathematical idea is global, but contracts are not. Canadian mortgages can contain explicit prepayment privileges and penalties for exceeding them. UK products may allow overpayments up to a stated limit and may apply an Early Repayment Charge. Australian guidance tells borrowers to check whether extra repayments are permitted and whether fees apply. Indian rules can differ by lender and rate structure, including protections for certain floating-rate loans. The calculator therefore asks users to enter known charges instead of inventing them.

Do not sacrifice financial resilience just to shorten the loan

Paying principal early can create a return roughly related to avoided loan interest, but the cash may become less accessible. Before sending every spare amount to the lender, consider emergency liquidity, higher-cost debts, near-term expenses and whether the loan has offset or redraw features. A mathematically faster payoff can still be a poor household decision if it leaves no buffer for a job loss or major repair.

How to test the idea with your own loan

  1. Enter the current balance, rate and remaining term rather than the original loan amount.
  2. Leave recurring extra payments off to confirm the baseline repayment path.
  3. Turn on an annual extra payment and enter an amount equal to one regular payment.
  4. Compare time saved and interest saved before any entered charges.
  5. Repeat with the payment made monthly or earlier in the year if that better matches your cash flow.
  6. Check the result against your lender’s rules before acting.

Common mistakes when using the “one extra payment” idea

The most common mistake is copying a viral example without matching its rate and term. Another is assuming the lender will automatically apply extra money to principal. Borrowers can also overlook penalties, use the original loan amount instead of the current balance, or assume a fixed rate when the real loan can reset. Finally, some people compare a prepayment with investing without considering liquidity, risk, tax and the fact that an investment return is uncertain while avoided contractual interest is mechanically different.

Frequently asked questions

Does one extra payment always save several years?

No. It can, especially on a long loan, but the result depends on rate, remaining term, payment amount and timing. Shorter loans or very low rates may save less time.

Should the extra payment be exactly the same as my regular payment?

No. That is simply an easy benchmark. Any additional principal can change the path. The calculator lets you test a smaller monthly amount, a larger annual lump sum or another cadence.

Is one annual payment better than twelve smaller extras?

If the total annual extra is identical and the smaller amounts reach principal earlier, the monthly pattern will usually have a slight mathematical advantage. If the lump sum is available earlier, the opposite can be true.

Will my lender show the same payoff date as Carrowmont?

Not necessarily. A lender may use daily interest, different compounding, settlement dates, fees and contract-specific rules. Treat the calculator as a planning estimate and request an official payoff or settlement quote when needed.
