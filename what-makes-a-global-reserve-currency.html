RESERVE CURRENCIES

What makes a currency a global reserve currency?

Reserve status is not awarded by one treaty or one trade contract. It is built from a large financial ecosystem that institutions can use in normal times and under stress.

30-second answer

A global reserve currency is a currency that central banks and other institutions are willing and able to hold in large amounts because there is a deep ecosystem around it: liquid assets, open markets, convertibility, trusted institutions, payment and funding networks, and widespread use in trade and finance. Reserve status is not created by one commodity contract or one bilateral trade agreement.

Start with the ecosystem

Reserve currency status is more than trade invoicing

A central bank does not hold a currency symbol in isolation. It needs assets denominated in that currency that can be bought, sold, pledged, transferred and used in stressed markets. That means reserve-currency status depends on the financial system around the currency as much as on the size of the issuing economy.

International use also creates network effects. If banks, companies, investors and governments already invoice, borrow, hedge and settle in one currency, using that same currency can reduce transaction frictions. Network effects can persist even when the issuing country's share of world output changes.

Current context: What IMF COFER does and does not measure

The IMF's Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves (COFER) dataset tracks disclosed foreign-exchange reserves by currency. The latest Q2 2026 data brief reported the U.S. dollar at 56.70% of global FX reserves, the euro at 20.60%, the Japanese yen at 4.96% and the Chinese renminbi at 2.11%. Those shares can move because of active portfolio decisions, bond-price changes and exchange-rate valuation effects.

Denominator rule: COFER excludes monetary gold, SDRs and reserve positions in the IMF. A chart showing “total official reserves including gold” uses a different denominator and cannot be compared directly with a COFER currency share without explanation.

Worked illustration: Why network effects matter

Imagine a global company that earns revenue in several countries, borrows from international banks, buys commodities under dollar contracts and hedges exchange-rate risk in deep derivatives markets. Even if it begins settling some regional trade in another currency, it may still keep substantial dollar liquidity because its debt, collateral and hedging relationships remain dollar-based.

A reserve manager faces a similar systems problem. Changing one invoicing choice is easier than replacing the surrounding stock of liquid assets, funding markets, payment rails, derivatives, custody arrangements and legal relationships. This is why reserve-currency transitions tend to be gradual rather than binary.

Economic size matters, but market openness matters too

A large economy can support an international currency, but size alone is not enough. IMF research on reserve currencies highlights global economic reach, credibility, financial-market depth and liquidity, policies that support or restrict internationalization, and inertia from past reserve usage. A currency can be important in trade while still having limited reserve use if capital cannot move freely or if safe-asset markets are not deep enough.

What can break the simple story?

“More trade in currency X means currency X will replace the dollar” skips most of the reserve ecosystem. Trade settlement is one channel. Reserve status also depends on investible assets, convertibility, market depth, collateral use, banking networks and confidence that funds remain accessible when needed.

Why reserve share can fall without a currency “collapse”

A currency's reserve share can decline gradually while it remains the largest reserve currency and remains heavily used in payments, funding and capital markets. Diversification into several smaller currencies can reduce concentration without producing one new dominant replacement. IMF research has documented that part of the long-run decline in the dollar share has flowed into a range of nontraditional reserve currencies rather than simply into one rival.

Valuation also matters. If one reserve currency appreciates against the reporting currency, its measured share can rise even without purchases. If bond prices fall as yields rise, the market value of reserve securities can decline. A quarter-to-quarter share change is therefore not automatically evidence of active buying or selling.

Checklist: Before believing a “reserve currency is being replaced” claim

  • Which dataset? Is it COFER FX reserves, total reserves including gold, trade invoicing, payments, bank lending or bond issuance?
  • Which denominator? Gold is not part of COFER's foreign-currency denominator.
  • Are valuation effects adjusted? Exchange rates and bond prices can change reported shares.
  • Is the shift broad or bilateral? One trade corridor does not establish a global replacement.
  • Can large investors access deep assets? Reserve managers need scale, liquidity and collateral utility.
  • Is the currency freely usable? Convertibility and capital-market access matter.
  • Are network effects changing? Funding, derivatives and settlement usage can persist long after trade shares move.

What would evidence of genuine displacement look like?

A stronger case for displacement would require persistent changes across several functions at once: a large shift in central-bank FX reserves after valuation adjustment; broader use in cross-border funding and trade finance; deep and open safe-asset markets; greater use as collateral and in derivatives; and durable private-sector adoption beyond a small set of bilateral agreements.

Key takeaway

Reserve-currency status is an ecosystem. The U.S. dollar's share can decline gradually and other currencies can gain roles without a sudden handover. To judge a headline, ask which financial function is changing and whether the supporting market infrastructure is changing with it.

Frequently asked questions

Does pricing oil in a currency make it a reserve currency?

It can support demand for that currency, but reserve status also requires liquid assets, convertibility, funding markets, custody, payments and institutional confidence.

Does COFER include central-bank gold?

No. IMF COFER covers foreign-exchange reserves by currency and excludes monetary gold, SDRs and reserve positions in the IMF from that FX-reserve classification.

Can a reserve currency lose share without losing dominance?

Yes. A leading currency can gradually lose percentage share while remaining much larger than alternatives and continuing to dominate other financial functions.

Why do network effects matter?

Using the same currency for contracts, funding, collateral, settlement and hedging can reduce friction. Once a network is established, participants have an incentive to keep using it until an alternative ecosystem becomes sufficiently useful.
