RETIREMENT SAVINGS GUIDE

How much should you have saved for retirement by 30, 40, 50 and 60?

Age-based benchmarks can be useful as a checkpoint, but they are not a substitute for a spending-based retirement plan.

A POPULAR CHECKPOINT

Benchmarks can tell you when to investigate, not exactly how much you need.

One of the most searched retirement questions is whether someone is ahead or behind for their age. That is understandable: a simple benchmark is easier to grasp than a full retirement model. The risk is treating a benchmark as a rule.

Fidelity publishes a widely cited guideline of roughly 1 times salary saved by age 30, 3 times by 40, 6 times by 50, 8 times by 60 and 10 times by 67. Fidelity itself describes these as goalposts based on assumptions, not guarantees or government standards.

Commonly cited Fidelity milestonesAge 30: about 1× annual salaryAge 40: about 3× annual salaryAge 50: about 6× annual salaryAge 60: about 8× annual salaryAge 67: about 10× annual salary

Why a salary multiple can mislead

Imagine two 45-year-olds earning the same amount. One owns a home with no mortgage and spends half of their income. The other rents in an expensive city, supports parents and children, and spends most of their income. The same salary-based benchmark says they need the same retirement savings even though their future cash-flow needs may be very different.

That is why a better second step is to estimate future spending directly.

What matters more than your age benchmark?

Retirement age. Retiring earlier reduces saving time and increases the likely withdrawal period.

Expected retirement spending. Housing, healthcare, travel and family support can make the retirement budget very different from working-life spending.

Income after retirement. Pensions, annuities, rental income or other dependable cash flows can reduce the amount the portfolio has to provide.

Existing assets outside retirement accounts. A household may have taxable investments, property or other assets earmarked for retirement that a workplace-account benchmark does not capture.

How to use an age benchmark productively

If you are below a benchmark, do not treat that as a verdict. Use it as a trigger to model the actual gap. If you are above it, do not assume the plan is complete. A large balance can still be inadequate if retirement is very early or spending is very high.

Better question: instead of asking only "How much should I have by my age?", ask "What future spending do I want to fund, and what path could reasonably support it?"

What to do in your 30s, 40s and 50s

Earlier in life, time is the strongest advantage because contributions have more years to compound. In mid-career, rising income can create room to increase contributions. Closer to retirement, the focus usually shifts toward the size of the spending need, the reliability of income sources, liquidity and the risk of large losses shortly before withdrawals begin.

Those priorities are more useful than trying to hit a single universal number on a birthday.

Frequently asked questions

What if I am below an age-based benchmark?

Treat it as a prompt to model the actual gap rather than as a pass-or-fail score. Increasing contributions, changing the retirement date, reducing future spending or using other assets can all affect the path.

What if I am above the benchmark?

Being above a benchmark does not prove the plan is fully funded. Early retirement, high spending, taxes, healthcare costs or limited retirement income can still require a larger portfolio.

Are retirement benchmarks the same in every country?

No. Tax systems, pensions, healthcare, household spending patterns and retirement ages differ. Salary multiples are best treated as broad educational checkpoints, not country-neutral rules.
