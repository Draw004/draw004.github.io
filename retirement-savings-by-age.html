RETIREMENT SAVINGS GUIDE

How much should you have saved for retirement by 30, 40, 50 and 60?

Age-based benchmarks can be useful as a checkpoint, but they are not a substitute for a spending-based retirement plan.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

Age-based retirement savings benchmarks are checkpoints, not pass/fail rules. They can show whether saving has broadly kept pace with income, but they cannot tell you whether your own retirement is funded because they usually assume a particular retirement age, savings rate, lifestyle and income-replacement pattern.

BEFORE YOU START

Benchmarks can tell you when to investigate, not exactly how much you need.

One of the most searched retirement questions is whether someone is ahead or behind for their age. That is understandable: a simple benchmark is easier to grasp than a full retirement model. The risk is treating a benchmark as a rule.

Fidelity publishes a widely cited guideline of roughly 1 times salary saved by age 30, 3 times by 40, 6 times by 50, 8 times by 60 and 10 times by 67. Fidelity itself describes these as goalposts based on assumptions, not guarantees or government standards.

Commonly cited Fidelity milestonesAge 30: about 1× annual salaryAge 40: about 3× annual salaryAge 50: about 6× annual salaryAge 60: about 8× annual salaryAge 67: about 10× annual salary

Why a salary multiple can mislead

Imagine two 45-year-olds earning the same amount. One owns a home with no mortgage and spends half of their income. The other rents in an expensive city, supports parents and children, and spends most of their income. The same salary-based benchmark says they need the same retirement savings even though their future cash-flow needs may be very different.

That is why a better second step is to estimate future spending directly.

What matters more than your age benchmark?

Retirement age. Retiring earlier reduces saving time and increases the likely withdrawal period.

Expected retirement spending. Housing, healthcare, travel and family support can make the retirement budget very different from working-life spending.

Income after retirement. Pensions, annuities, rental income or other dependable cash flows can reduce the amount the portfolio has to provide.

Existing assets outside retirement accounts. A household may have taxable investments, property or other assets earmarked for retirement that a workplace-account benchmark does not capture.

How to use an age benchmark productively

If you are below a benchmark, do not treat that as a verdict. Use it as a trigger to model the actual gap. If you are above it, do not assume the plan is complete. A large balance can still be inadequate if retirement is very early or spending is very high.

Better question: instead of asking only "How much should I have by my age?", ask "What future spending do I want to fund, and what path could reasonably support it?"

What to do in your 30s, 40s and 50s

Earlier in life, time is the strongest advantage because contributions have more years to compound. In mid-career, rising income can create room to increase contributions. Closer to retirement, the focus usually shifts toward the size of the spending need, the reliability of income sources, liquidity and the risk of large losses shortly before withdrawals begin.

Those priorities are more useful than trying to hit a single universal number on a birthday.

What a salary multiple actually measures

A benchmark such as “X times salary by age Y” compares accumulated retirement assets with current annual income. It is easy to understand and useful for spotting a large gap, but salary is not the same as retirement spending. The relationship between the two changes with taxes, housing, dependants, savings rate and lifestyle.

One widely cited benchmark—and its assumptions

Fidelity’s September 2026 guideline suggests roughly 1× salary by age 30, 3× by 40, 6× by 50, 8× by 60 and 10× by 67. Fidelity describes these as aspirational milestones, not personal guarantees. They are best used as a comparison point, especially for readers whose retirement age and lifestyle differ from the assumptions behind them.

Why two people the same age can need different multiples

  • One plans to retire at 55 while the other plans to work until 67.
  • One expects a strong inflation-linked pension; the other must fund nearly all spending from investments.
  • One owns a mortgage-free home; the other expects high housing costs.
  • One wants to preserve a legacy; the other is comfortable spending down principal.
  • One has high healthcare/care exposure or family-support commitments.

Build a personal benchmark from your own target

Instead of comparing only with salary, estimate the retirement portfolio you need and divide it by the number of years remaining. Then compare the projected value of current assets plus future contributions with that target. A person can be “behind” a generic age multiple but on track for a modest, later retirement—or ahead of the benchmark but underfunded for an expensive early retirement.

Being behind is information, not a diagnosis

A gap can be addressed through several levers: contribution rate, retirement date, target spending, current asset allocation and future income. Increasing expected returns on paper is not a dependable way to close a gap because the higher return generally comes with higher uncertainty.

The decade before retirement deserves a different review

As withdrawals approach, the question shifts from “How much have I accumulated?” to “How resilient is the income plan?” A portfolio can meet an age benchmark and still be vulnerable if it is highly concentrated, illiquid or dependent on selling risky assets immediately after a market decline.

Turn a benchmark gap into an action schedule

If a benchmark suggests a large shortfall, calculate how much of it could realistically be addressed by higher contributions, a later retirement date, lower retirement spending or improved tax/fee efficiency. Avoid the tempting but unreliable fix of assuming a dramatically higher return. A catch-up plan should change controllable variables first.

Compare savings rate and funded status together

A 45-year-old with a modest balance but a 30% savings rate may be improving quickly; another with a high balance and near-zero saving may be coasting on past progress. The current balance is a snapshot; the contribution trajectory shows whether the gap is closing.

Benchmark assets consistently

Decide whether employer pensions, defined-benefit entitlements, business value and home equity are included. Generic salary multiples usually refer to retirement savings, not every household asset. Inconsistent counting can make comparisons meaningless.

Frequently asked questions

Should I panic if I am below an age benchmark?

No. First translate your own retirement date, spending and income sources into a target. The benchmark can prompt a review, but it is not a personalized funding test.

Can I compare retirement savings with gross salary?

That is how many salary-multiple benchmarks are framed, but the more useful planning variable is the spending your portfolio must fund after other income sources.

Does a pension mean I need less invested?

Potentially. Reliable pension income reduces the spending burden on the portfolio, especially if it keeps pace with inflation. Model the actual terms rather than assuming all pensions behave the same way.

What matters most in my 50s and 60s?

Contribution rate still matters, but retirement timing, asset allocation, liquidity, debt, healthcare planning and sequence risk become increasingly important as withdrawals approach.
