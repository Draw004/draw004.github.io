Compounding makes time an important part of every long-term projection.

When growth is repeatedly applied to a changing balance, later years build on earlier years. The same mathematics affects investment growth assumptions and future prices.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide

Direct answer

Time changes compounding non-linearly. One extra year does not add the same amount as the previous year because growth is applied to a changing balance. That is why long-horizon projections accelerate—and why assumptions that are only slightly too optimistic can create very large differences decades later.

What compounding means

If an amount earns a return and that return remains invested, the next period's return can apply to both the original amount and prior gains. Over time, this creates growth on growth.

Inflation compounds in the same mathematical way. A future price increase is applied to the previous year's higher price, which is why long-term future costs can rise substantially even when the annual percentage looks modest.

Time and rate work together

The longer the time horizon, the more sensitive a projection becomes to its rate assumption. This is why changing a long-term return or inflation assumption by only one or two percentage points can materially change a projected future value.

Illustrative growth of 100 over 20 years:At 5% annual growth: about 265At 7% annual growth: about 387At 9% annual growth: about 560

Regular contributions add another layer

When you contribute regularly, each contribution has a different amount of time to compound. Earlier contributions generally have more time in the model than later contributions. This is why a monthly savings projection is not the same as multiplying one monthly contribution by the number of months.

A smooth model is not a smooth real-world path

Planning tools often use a constant annual return assumption because it makes the model understandable. Real investment returns are uneven and can include losses. Two portfolios with the same long-run average return can experience different outcomes depending on the order of returns and the timing of withdrawals.

Use compounding as a planning mechanism, not a promise. A rate entered in a calculator is an assumption used to explore a scenario.

Compounding means growth on previous growth

With simple interest, returns are calculated only on the original principal. With compounding, each period’s return becomes part of the balance that can earn future returns. When regular contributions are added, every contribution starts its own compounding clock.

Single lump sumFuture value = Present value × (1 + return)^years
Future cost under inflationFuture cost = Current cost × (1 + inflation)^years

The same exponential maths works for both wealth and prices

Compounding is not inherently good or bad. Investment growth can compound in your favour; inflation can compound against purchasing power; interest on expensive debt can compound against the borrower. Planning becomes clearer when you recognize that all three are versions of the same exponential process.

Why small assumption changes create big long-term differences

A difference between 7% and 8% sounds like one percentage point. Over 30 years, however, a one-time amount compounds to roughly 7.61 times at 7% and 10.06 times at 8%. This is why long-term calculators should show ranges and avoid excessive precision in the final answer.

Time can reduce the monthly contribution required for a goal

For a fixed target, extending the horizon gives both the current balance and future contributions more time to grow. It does not guarantee a higher return, but it reduces the amount of the target that must come from each monthly contribution.

Useful mental models

  • Think in multipliers, not just percentages. A 7% annual assumption over 30 years is not “210%”; it compounds to much more than that.
  • Separate nominal and real growth. If returns are 8% and inflation is 5%, purchasing power does not grow by the full 8%.
  • Treat long-horizon decimal precision with suspicion. A projection to the nearest currency unit is mathematically possible but economically misleading.
  • Use time as a planning lever early; later, contribution increases may have to do more of the work.

Compounding can work backward as well as forward

Positive returns compound wealth, but fees, inflation and debt interest also compound. A 20% gain after a 20% loss does not restore the original value because the gain is applied to a smaller base. Understanding this asymmetry is as important as memorizing exponential growth charts.

The Rule of 72 is useful—but only as a rough mental model

Dividing 72 by an annual growth rate gives an approximate doubling time for moderate positive rates. At 8%, the estimate is about nine years. It is not a substitute for exact compounding, and it is especially rough at very high or negative rates.

Real compounding is what matters for long-term purchasing power

An account that compounds at 7% while inflation compounds at 5% does not create 7% annual improvement in lifestyle. The exact real rate is about 1.90%. Long horizons magnify even small differences between nominal and real growth.

Frequently asked questions

Is a compound-growth projection a forecast?

No. It shows what would happen under a stated constant rate. Market returns vary, so actual outcomes will differ.

Why do long-term projections look so sensitive to the return assumption?

Because each year’s difference becomes part of the base for future years. Small annual differences compound repeatedly.

Does inflation compound the same way?

Yes mathematically. If prices rise by a constant percentage each year, the future cost compounds on the prior year’s higher price level.
