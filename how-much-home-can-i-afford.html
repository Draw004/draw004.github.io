HOME AFFORDABILITY GUIDE

How much home can I afford?

Start with the monthly life you want to keep after buying, then work backward to a home price.

By CarrowmontUpdated 19 Oct 2026Educational guideMethodology
TRY THE NUMBERS YOURSELFOpen the Home Affordability Calculator→

Use your own income, expenses, debts, deposit and chosen cash-flow buffer to test what may fit your household budget.

Direct answer

A home price is more likely to fit your budget when the full monthly housing cost still leaves room for essential spending, existing debt, savings and a cash buffer. Start with what your household can comfortably carry each month, then work backward to a loan amount and home price. A lender may approve a different amount.

Start with your monthly life, not a maximum loan

“How much home can I afford?” sounds like one number, but it is really a cash-flow question. A home does not sit outside the rest of your financial life. The payment has to coexist with food, transport, childcare, insurance, debt repayments, savings, irregular bills and the ordinary things you still want to do after buying.

A useful affordability estimate therefore starts with monthly take-home income and subtracts the commitments you want to preserve. The amount left for housing is more informative than an income multiple on its own because two households with the same income can have very different expenses, debts and priorities.

A simple cash-flow framework

Room for the loan paymentTake-home income − essential spending − existing debt − protected savings − free-cash buffer − other ownership costsThe remainder is the amount available for modeled principal and interest. If it is zero or negative, the household assumptions do not currently leave room for a positive loan payment.

Carrowmont then uses the interest rate and loan term to convert that monthly payment room into a standard amortizing loan amount. The deposit or down payment is added to that loan amount to produce a planning home price. This is a household-budget model, not an underwriting model.

What should count as essential spending?

Use the spending that is likely to continue after you move. Think about food, transport, utilities not already included in housing costs, childcare, insurance, healthcare, family support and other commitments that are difficult to pause. Do not make the home look affordable by pretending normal life will become much cheaper unless you have a realistic reason.

If some spending is optional, you can test two versions. One can use your current lifestyle. Another can use a leaner budget you would genuinely be willing to live with. Seeing the difference is more useful than silently assuming every flexible expense will disappear.

Protect the savings you do not want the house to consume

Buying a home can compete with emergency savings, retirement investing and other goals. If a monthly saving or investing amount matters to you, include it before calculating the room for housing. That makes the home price smaller, but it also makes the result more honest about the rest of your plan.

The same idea applies to a free-cash buffer. A budget that works only when every month is perfect can feel fragile. Keeping money unallocated gives you room for repairs, travel, irregular bills or simple breathing space. Carrowmont lets you choose that buffer instead of declaring one universal percentage “safe.”

Do not forget the costs outside the mortgage

A mortgage or home-loan payment is only one part of ownership. Depending on where you live and the property, you may also face property taxes or local rates, building or homeowners insurance, service charges, HOA or strata fees, routine maintenance and other recurring costs. Some places also have material one-off purchase costs such as stamp duty, registration, legal fees or closing costs.

Carrowmont does not guess those country-specific amounts. Enter recurring costs that are relevant to the home you are considering and plan separately for one-off purchase costs. If you enter zero, treat the headline as incomplete until you have checked whether zero is realistic.

A worked example

Imagine a household has 10,000 of monthly take-home income. Essential spending is 4,000, existing debt is 500, protected savings are 1,000, the household wants 1,000 left free, and other ownership costs are estimated at 500 a month. That leaves 3,000 for principal and interest.

If the chosen loan assumptions can support a loan of 400,000 from that payment room and the household has a 100,000 deposit, the planning home price would be about 500,000. The exact loan amount depends on the rate and term. The point of the example is not the currency or the specific price. It is the order of the decision: protect the rest of the monthly plan first, then see what housing fits inside it.

What happens when the interest rate changes?

For the same monthly payment, a higher interest rate supports a smaller loan. That means the same household cash flow can support a lower home price unless the deposit increases or another part of the budget changes. A lower rate does the opposite, but it should not automatically be treated as permission to spend the entire difference.

It can be useful to test a somewhat higher rate as a personal planning check. This is not a prediction and not a lender stress test. It simply shows how sensitive the plan is to the rate assumption you entered.

What happens when the term changes?

A longer term usually reduces the monthly payment for the same loan, which can make a larger loan fit the same monthly budget. But the debt remains for longer and total interest can be higher. A shorter term usually needs a larger monthly payment but can reduce the time spent paying interest. Affordability is therefore not only about squeezing the payment under a monthly limit.

Why the lender may give you a different number

A lender can look at verified income, credit history, debt, property value, product rules, loan-to-value limits, stress rates, documentation and local regulation. Those rules vary by lender and country. A household-planning calculator should not pretend to reproduce them.

This difference matters in both directions. A lender may be willing to lend more than feels comfortable in your budget, or it may lend less than your household cash flow appears to support. Treat the two questions separately: “What fits my life?” and “What will a lender approve?”

A practical affordability checklist

  1. Use take-home income you can reasonably rely on.
  2. Keep normal essential spending in the plan.
  3. Include existing debt repayments until they really end.
  4. Protect savings or investing you do not want the home purchase to crowd out.
  5. Choose a monthly buffer rather than assuming every spare unit can go to housing.
  6. Add realistic recurring ownership costs.
  7. Test the rate and term, then compare a higher-rate scenario.
  8. Keep enough cash for purchase costs, moving, repairs and emergency reserves.
  9. Compare the result with renting and with the lender’s actual written terms.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the maximum loan: approval capacity is not the same as household comfort.
  • Ignoring ownership costs: the loan payment is not the full housing budget.
  • Using every unit of monthly surplus: that leaves no room for surprises.
  • Counting a deposit twice: the deposit reduces the loan needed; it is not also monthly income.
  • Assuming today’s rate will never change: variable rates and future refinancing can change payments.
  • Forgetting other goals: a home can be important without becoming the only financial objective.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one correct percentage of income to spend on housing?

No universal percentage fits every household or country. Income stability, debt, taxes, family costs, savings goals and local housing expenses can all change what feels manageable.

Should I include rent when estimating future home affordability?

Usually not as an ongoing expense if the rent will end when you buy. But keep any overlap period, moving costs or temporary double housing costs in your separate purchase plan.

Does the calculator tell me what a bank will approve?

No. It estimates household affordability from the assumptions you enter. Lender approval is a separate process.

What if my income changes every month?

Use a conservative recurring amount and test a weaker-income scenario. A variable-income household may want a larger buffer before committing to a fixed housing payment.
