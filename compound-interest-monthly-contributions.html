COMPOUND INTEREST GUIDE

How does compound interest work with monthly contributions?

Compounding becomes much more powerful to understand when you separate your own contributions from the growth applied to the balance.

THE CORE IDEA

Compounding means growth can be applied to earlier growth as well as to your original money.

If 100 grows by 5%, it becomes 105. If the next period's 5% applies to 105 rather than only to the original 100, the new balance becomes 110.25. That extra 0.25 is the simplest illustration of growth on prior growth.

Monthly contributions add another layer because every contribution enters the calculation at a different time. The earliest contributions have the longest period to compound; the last contributions have the shortest.

Illustrative growth of 100 over 20 yearsAt 5% annual growth: about 265At 7% annual growth: about 387At 9% annual growth: about 560

Why a monthly contribution calculator is not simple multiplication

If you invest 500 a month for 20 years, your own contributions are 120,000. The ending value is not found by multiplying 500 by 240 and then applying one return to the whole amount, because the first 500 was invested for almost 20 years while the last 500 was invested for only a short period.

A future-value calculator models each contribution at its appropriate point in time.

Rate conventions matter

Two calculators can produce slightly different results even when the headline annual rate is the same. One may divide an annual nominal rate by 12; another may convert an annual effective rate to an equivalent monthly compound rate. Contribution timing can also differ: beginning of month versus end of month.

That does not automatically make one calculator wrong. It means methodology should be visible.

Time can matter more than a small change in contribution

A contribution started earlier has more compounding periods. This is why delaying a long-term goal can require a meaningfully larger monthly amount later, even when the target itself has not changed.

Important: a constant return entered into a calculator is a modelling assumption. Real market returns vary from year to year and can include losses.

Why you should test more than one return assumption

Long time horizons magnify small differences in assumed returns. Instead of treating one rate as a prediction, compare a lower, middle and higher scenario. If the plan only works at the highest assumption, you have learned something important about its sensitivity.

Compound interest and inflation use similar mathematics

Compounding is not only about investments. Prices can compound too. If inflation repeats year after year, future costs build on already higher prices. That is why a long-term goal should usually distinguish between investment growth and price growth.

Frequently asked questions

Does compounding happen monthly in real investments?

It depends on the investment and how returns are measured. Calculators often convert an annual assumption into periodic growth for modelling. Real market returns are uneven rather than a smooth monthly rate.

Why do two calculators give different answers?

Common reasons include different rate conventions, contribution timing, compounding frequency, fees, taxes and rounding. Check the methodology before comparing results.

Is a higher assumed return always better for planning?

A higher return can make the required contribution look smaller, but it also makes the plan more dependent on investment performance. Testing a lower-return scenario is a useful robustness check.
