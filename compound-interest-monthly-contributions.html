COMPOUND INTEREST GUIDE

How does compound interest work with monthly contributions?

Compounding becomes much more powerful to understand when you separate your own contributions from the growth applied to the balance.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Model monthly investment growth→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

With monthly contributions, compounding works on many separate cash flows. Each contribution earns returns only from the date it enters the account, so the earliest contributions have the longest time to grow. The result is an annuity-style future-value calculation, not simply “monthly deposit × compound rate.”

BEFORE YOU START

Compounding means growth can be applied to earlier growth as well as to your original money.

If 100 grows by 5%, it becomes 105. If the next period's 5% applies to 105 rather than only to the original 100, the new balance becomes 110.25. That extra 0.25 is the simplest illustration of growth on prior growth.

Monthly contributions add another layer because every contribution enters the calculation at a different time. The earliest contributions have the longest period to compound; the last contributions have the shortest.

Illustrative growth of 100 over 20 yearsAt 5% annual growth: about 265At 7% annual growth: about 387At 9% annual growth: about 560

Why a monthly contribution calculator is not simple multiplication

If you invest 500 a month for 20 years, your own contributions are 120,000. The ending value is not found by multiplying 500 by 240 and then applying one return to the whole amount, because the first 500 was invested for almost 20 years while the last 500 was invested for only a short period.

A future-value calculator models each contribution at its appropriate point in time.

Rate conventions matter

Two calculators can produce slightly different results even when the headline annual rate is the same. One may divide an annual nominal rate by 12; another may convert an annual effective rate to an equivalent monthly compound rate. Contribution timing can also differ: beginning of month versus end of month.

That does not automatically make one calculator wrong. It means methodology should be visible.

Time can matter more than a small change in contribution

A contribution started earlier has more compounding periods. This is why delaying a long-term goal can require a meaningfully larger monthly amount later, even when the target itself has not changed.

Important: a constant return entered into a calculator is a modelling assumption. Real market returns vary from year to year and can include losses.

Why you should test more than one return assumption

Long time horizons magnify small differences in assumed returns. Instead of treating one rate as a prediction, compare a lower, middle and higher scenario. If the plan only works at the highest assumption, you have learned something important about its sensitivity.

Compound interest and inflation use similar mathematics

Compounding is not only about investments. Prices can compound too. If inflation repeats year after year, future costs build on already higher prices. That is why a long-term goal should usually distinguish between investment growth and price growth.

There are two engines: contributions and growth

Your ending balance is the sum of what you put in plus the growth earned on the starting balance and each contribution. Early in the plan, new contributions often dominate. Later, if returns have been positive, growth on the accumulated balance can become the larger annual driver.

Future value with end-of-month contributionsFV = PV(1+r)^n + PMT × ((1+r)^n − 1) / rFor beginning-of-month contributions, the contribution term is multiplied by (1+r) because every contribution receives one additional month of growth.

Compounding frequency and contribution frequency are different ideas

A product may quote an annual return while contributions arrive monthly, weekly or irregularly. A calculator must convert the annual assumption into a periodic rate consistently. Simply dividing an annual effective return by 12 is not always equivalent to the product’s actual return convention, so projections should state their method.

Why starting early changes the contribution burden₹10,000/month for 10 years at a smooth 8% grows to about ₹18.29 lakh.The same ₹10,000/month for 20 years grows to about ₹58.90 lakh.Total contributions only double from ₹12 lakh to ₹24 lakh, but the projected ending value grows by much more because the earlier money compounds longer.

The smooth-return assumption is the biggest simplification

Real investment returns vary from month to month. During accumulation, two paths with the same average return can still produce different results because contributions are made along the way. Lower prices early can allow recurring contributions to buy more units, while strong returns later act on a larger accumulated balance. This is one reason a deterministic compound-interest projection should be treated as an illustration rather than a forecast.

Fees, taxes and inflation compound too

A recurring fee that looks small each year reduces the amount that remains invested, so its effect also compounds over long horizons. Inflation works in the opposite direction on purchasing power: it raises the future amount needed to buy the same basket of goods. A useful plan therefore distinguishes gross return, net return and real return.

Approximate real returnReal return ≈ (1 + nominal return) / (1 + inflation) − 1

Four questions to ask when a projection looks surprisingly large

  1. How much of the ending value is my own contribution?
  2. What annual return is being assumed, and is it before or after fees?
  3. Is the return applied consistently to the contribution frequency?
  4. What would the result look like after adjusting for inflation?

Compounding has three clocks: contribution timing, return timing and time horizon

Monthly contributions do not all compound for the same length of time. The first deposit may grow for decades while the final deposit grows for only one month. This is why a formula using one lump sum cannot accurately represent a recurring plan. It is also why moving a contribution earlier in the month or year has only a small effect compared with extending the horizon by years.

Quoted annual return and monthly rate are not always interchangeable

A calculator may divide an annual nominal rate by 12, while an investment’s realized annual return compounds irregularly. An effective annual rate converts periodic compounding into one annual growth figure. For educational projections, the convention should be stated clearly so users can reproduce the math.

Fees and taxes compound too—against you

A recurring annual fee reduces the base on which future returns compound. Over a long horizon, a seemingly small difference between gross and net return can create a large ending-value gap. Taxes can have a similar effect depending on the account and jurisdiction.

  • Use net-of-fee return assumptions when possible.
  • Keep contribution frequency consistent with the formula.
  • Treat smooth compounding as a planning abstraction, not a market forecast.

Frequently asked questions

Is compound interest the same as investment return?

Not exactly. Compound interest is a mathematical mechanism. Market investments generate variable returns, which can be compounded but are not guaranteed interest.

Does contributing at the beginning of the month matter?

Mathematically it gives each contribution one additional period to grow. Over long periods the difference is usually smaller than the effects of contribution size, time and actual returns, but a calculator should be internally consistent.

Why can two calculators show different results?

They may use different return conventions, compounding frequencies, contribution timing, fees or rounding. Check the methodology rather than assuming one is wrong from the ending number alone.
