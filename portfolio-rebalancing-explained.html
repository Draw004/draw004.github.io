Portfolio rebalancing: What is it, and when does it matter?

Rebalancing is not a forecast about which asset will win next. It is a maintenance decision that brings a portfolio back toward the risk mix chosen for the goal.

By CarrowmontPublished 25 November 2026 · Reviewed 25 November 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy

30-second answer

A portfolio drifts when some assets rise or fall faster than others. Rebalancing means adjusting the holdings back toward the target asset mix. The purpose is risk control and plan discipline, not market timing. You can rebalance with new contributions, by selling and buying, or with a combination; taxes, transaction costs and account rules may affect which method is practical.

Tax on sales, transaction costs, retirement-account rules and available investment products differ by country. Carrowmont explains the planning mechanics; check local tax and account rules before acting.

Why portfolios drift

Suppose a portfolio starts at 60% shares and 40% bonds. If shares rise much faster, the portfolio might become 70/30 without the investor choosing that change. The expected return may be different, but so is the risk. Rebalancing is the process of deciding whether to restore the original mix.

Drift can happen in the other direction too. After a market fall, the share allocation can become smaller. Restoring the target may require adding to the part that has recently performed poorly, which can feel uncomfortable. That discomfort is one reason a written policy can be useful.

Rebalancing is different from market timing

Market timing tries to predict what will rise or fall next. Rebalancing starts from a target allocation chosen for the goal and restores that mix when the portfolio moves away from it. The decision rule is based on the plan, not on a forecast.

Four common ways to rebalance

MethodHow it worksMain trade-off
New contributionsDirect fresh money toward underweight assetsCan reduce selling and tax friction but may be slow for a large portfolio
WithdrawalsTake spending from overweight assetsUseful in drawdown but depends on what you need to sell
Sell and buySell overweight assets and buy underweight assetsFastest reset but may create tax, fees or bid-ask costs
HybridUse cash flows first, then trade only the remaining gapBalances precision with lower turnover

Calendar vs threshold rebalancing

A calendar approach reviews the portfolio at intervals such as every six or 12 months. A threshold approach reviews when an asset class moves a defined amount away from target. Neither method guarantees a better return. The purpose is to create a repeatable discipline and avoid constant trading.

Reviewing too frequently can create unnecessary transactions, taxes and attention. Reviewing too rarely can allow the portfolio to carry a very different risk profile from the one you intended.

A worked example

Imagine a 100,000 portfolio targeted at 60% shares and 40% bonds. After a strong share-market period it becomes 70,000 shares and 35,000 bonds, worth 105,000 in total. The actual mix is about 66.7% shares and 33.3% bonds.

A 60/40 target on 105,000 would be 63,000 shares and 42,000 bonds. A full rebalance would therefore shift about 7,000 from shares toward bonds. But if 5,000 of new money is about to be invested, part of the gap could be filled without selling.

When the target itself should change

Rebalancing restores a target. It does not tell you whether the target is still appropriate. A genuine change in time horizon, financial situation, goal, spending need or capacity for loss may justify changing the target allocation first. That is a strategic decision, not routine rebalancing.

Taxes and costs matter

Selling can create capital gains or other tax consequences in some accounts and countries. Funds and platforms may have transaction charges, spreads or redemption rules. A portfolio can therefore be “more perfectly balanced” but economically worse after unnecessary costs. Consider cash-flow rebalancing and tax-advantaged accounts where applicable.

Rebalancing during a market fall

A falling asset can become underweight. Rebalancing may therefore require buying more of something that has recently fallen. That can be emotionally difficult. The important question is whether the original allocation still fits the goal and your risk capacity—not whether the recent return looks attractive or frightening.

A practical review checklist

  1. Write down the target allocation for this specific goal.
  2. Calculate the current allocation using current market values.
  3. Measure the drift in percentage points.
  4. Check whether the goal, time horizon or risk capacity has changed.
  5. Use new contributions or withdrawals to reduce drift where practical.
  6. Estimate tax and trading costs before selling solely to rebalance.
  7. Record the decision so the next review uses the same discipline.

Common mistakes

  • Rebalancing because of a market prediction: That changes the purpose from maintenance to timing.
  • Ignoring taxes and fees: Turnover is not free.
  • Using one target for every goal: Goals with different horizons can need different risk mixes.
  • Rebalancing constantly: Small daily movements usually do not require action.
  • Never revisiting the target: The correct mix can change when the financial plan changes.

Frequently asked questions

Does rebalancing improve returns?

Not necessarily. Its primary purpose is to keep portfolio risk closer to the intended allocation. Depending on market paths and costs, returns can be higher or lower than a portfolio that was not rebalanced.

How often should I rebalance?

There is no universal schedule. Calendar and threshold methods are both common. Choose a rule you can follow without unnecessary turnover.

Can I rebalance without selling?

Often, yes. New contributions, dividends or withdrawals can be directed toward underweight or overweight assets.

Should I rebalance after every market move?

No. Rebalancing is usually an occasional maintenance process, not a response to every headline.
