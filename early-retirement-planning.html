Early retirement: What changes when you stop working 5 or 10 years earlier?

Early retirement creates a double effect: Fewer years to build the portfolio and more years that the portfolio may need to support spending.

By CarrowmontPublished 18 November 2026 · Reviewed 18 November 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy
Want to test a different retirement age?Open the Retirement Planner→

Compare retirement ages using the same spending, savings, contribution and return assumptions so you can isolate what moving the date changes.

30-second answer

Retiring earlier usually makes the plan harder in two directions at once: You stop contributions and employment income sooner, while withdrawals may need to last longer. It can also create a bridge before public pension, employer pension, healthcare or retirement-account access begins. The right test is not “Can I reach one magic number?” but “Can this cash-flow system survive the longer transition?”

Pension eligibility ages, retirement-account access, healthcare systems and tax rules are country-specific. Treat any country example as an illustration and verify the rules that apply to you.

The double effect of moving the date forward

Suppose your current plan assumes retirement in 20 years. Moving retirement five years earlier means 15 years of contributions instead of 20 and roughly five extra years in which employment income is no longer supporting spending. Moving it ten years earlier can remove half the remaining accumulation period.

This is why early retirement cannot be modeled only by multiplying annual expenses by a withdrawal multiple. The accumulation phase and the withdrawal phase both change.

Illustration: The lost compounding years can be large

Assume you contribute 1,000 per month at a smooth 5% annual return, with contributions at month end. Ignore taxes and fees. This is not a forecast; it isolates the effect of time.

Retiring five years earlier in this simplified example reduces both contributions and the time those contributions compound. Retiring ten years earlier changes the accumulation result much more than simply subtracting ten years of contributions.

The spending horizon also gets longer

If you retire at 60 instead of 65, a plan that needed to cover 30 years may now need to cover roughly 35. Retiring at 55 could extend the portfolio-funded period further. Life expectancy is uncertain, so the plan should not rely on a precise death date. Test longer horizons and a margin for longevity.

More years also mean more opportunities for inflation, market downturns, healthcare costs and life changes. That does not make early retirement impossible; it makes flexibility more valuable.

Build the bridge before pensions and benefits begin

Early retirement can create years in which the portfolio must fund nearly all spending before another income source starts. In the UK, for example, MoneyHelper notes that retiring early can mean a smaller workplace pension and that State Pension does not begin immediately. In the United States, Social Security can generally start earlier than full retirement age but the monthly benefit can be reduced; for people with full retirement age 67, claiming at 62 can mean a reduction of up to 30% under current rules.

The exact ages and reductions differ by system. Model each income source with its own start date rather than assuming “retirement income” all begins on day one.

Healthcare can be a bridge cost too

In countries where employer coverage ends when work ends or public coverage begins only at a later age, healthcare can be a major early-retirement expense. Even where public healthcare exists, private insurance, medicines, long-term care or out-of-pocket costs can change. Put healthcare into the cash-flow model instead of treating it as an invisible footnote.

Sequence risk starts earlier because withdrawals start earlier

A portfolio that falls just after retirement is dealing with two pressures: Lower asset prices and withdrawals funding living costs. Selling assets after a decline can leave fewer units to recover when markets rebound. Early retirement lengthens the period over which this risk can matter.

Possible responses include a larger cash reserve, flexible discretionary spending, part-time income, a lower initial withdrawal, or a portfolio allocation designed for the withdrawal phase. None removes risk; each changes where the risk sits.

Do not assume spending stays flat

Early retirement can reduce commuting and work-related costs but increase travel, hobbies or private healthcare. Later in life, spending can shift again. Build a baseline essential budget and a flexible lifestyle budget so the plan knows what could be cut temporarily after a weak market year.

That gap can change each year as pensions start, mortgages finish, dependants become independent or healthcare costs change.

Run three retirement ages with the same assumptions

A useful comparison keeps everything except retirement age constant. Test your planned age, five years earlier and ten years earlier. For each, compare:

  • Portfolio at retirement.
  • Years of contributions remaining.
  • Years until each pension or benefit begins.
  • Annual essential and flexible spending.
  • Initial withdrawal rate or withdrawal amount.
  • How the plan behaves under lower returns, higher inflation and weak returns early in retirement.

Then ask what change closes the gap: Working one more year, saving more now, reducing retirement spending, earning part-time income, delaying a benefit claim or changing the goal age.

Early retirement checklist

  1. Set the proposed retirement date, not only an age.
  2. Build an annual spending estimate in today’s money and decide how inflation is modeled.
  3. List each pension, benefit and annuity with its earliest and preferred start date.
  4. Check healthcare and insurance costs between work and later coverage.
  5. Model taxes and account-access rules for the bridge years.
  6. Stress test at least one poor early-return sequence.
  7. Define which spending can fall temporarily.
  8. Keep an emergency reserve outside the long-term withdrawal assumptions.
  9. Re-run the plan annually and after major market, income or family changes.

Common mistakes

  • Using the same retirement corpus target after moving retirement ten years earlier.
  • Ignoring the years before public or employer benefits start.
  • Assuming healthcare costs remain identical after leaving employment.
  • Using a smooth average return and never testing a bad first five years.
  • Counting home equity or other assets as spendable without a plan to access them.
  • Making every retirement expense non-negotiable and leaving no flexibility after market declines.

Frequently asked questions

Does retiring five years earlier require exactly five extra years of expenses?

No. You also lose five years of contributions and compounding, and pensions or benefits may start at different ages. The full effect can be much larger than five years of spending.

Can part-time work materially change the plan?

Yes. Even modest earned income can reduce portfolio withdrawals during the most sequence-sensitive early years and may preserve access to benefits in some systems. Model it explicitly rather than assuming it will be available.

Should I use the 4% rule for early retirement?

A fixed rule can be a starting reference, not a guarantee. A longer horizon, fees, taxes, inflation, asset allocation and return sequence can all change sustainability. Stress testing is more useful than treating one percentage as universal.

What is the biggest early-retirement lever?

Usually the retirement date itself is powerful because one additional working year can add contributions, compounding and earnings while removing a year of withdrawals. Spending flexibility and savings rate are also major levers.
