FINANCIAL INDEPENDENCE GUIDE

What is your financial independence number?

Your financial independence number is a spending-based estimate of the portfolio that may be needed to support a chosen level of spending without relying on salary.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

A financial-independence (FI) number is the portfolio size intended to support the spending that remains after reliable non-portfolio income. The familiar “annual expenses × 25” estimate is simply the reciprocal of a 4% starting withdrawal rate. It is a useful first approximation, but a real FI target should reflect horizon, taxes, spending flexibility, one-off goals and the possibility that the target itself changes over time.

BEFORE YOU START

Financial independence is easier to model from spending than from income.

The core question is not "How much do I earn?" but "How much would my portfolio need to provide each year if work income stopped?" That makes annual spending the natural starting point.

A simple mathematical shortcut is annual spending divided by a chosen withdrawal-rate assumption. At 4%, that is 25 times annual spending. At 3.5%, it is about 28.6 times. At 3%, it is about 33.3 times.

Example with annual spending of 60,000At 4%: target about 1.50 millionAt 3.5%: target about 1.71 millionAt 3%: target about 2.00 million

These are arithmetic scenarios, not recommended withdrawal rates. The sustainable amount depends on investment returns, inflation, taxes, fees, time horizon and how spending changes over time.

Why lower spending has a double effect

Reducing recurring spending can increase the amount available to save today and reduce the portfolio target needed later. That is why the savings rate is central to many financial-independence discussions.

Do not count every asset automatically

A home you plan to live in may be valuable but may not be available to fund spending unless you intend to sell, downsize or borrow against it. Separate assets that are genuinely available for financial independence from assets that serve another purpose.

Income after financial independence can reduce the target

Part-time work, pensions, rental income or other dependable cash flows can reduce the amount that the portfolio needs to provide. A good model lets you include those separately instead of pretending the portfolio must fund every expense forever.

Inflation still matters

If financial independence is years away, today's spending should be translated into future money or the model should work consistently in today's purchasing-power terms. Mixing the two can distort the target.

Use the number as a scenario, not a finish line. Test several spending levels and withdrawal assumptions so you can see what actually drives the target.

Financial independence is not identical to retirement

Someone can be financially independent and still choose to work. Retirement planning also tends to include a specific retirement age, pension timing, later-life healthcare costs and a finite planning horizon. The concepts overlap, but the questions are not identical.

Start with the spending the portfolio actually needs to fund

Do not automatically multiply current salary. Build a sustainable lifestyle budget, add irregular expenses, taxes and healthcare, then subtract dependable income. The FI number is therefore a liability-matching concept: how much capital is needed to support a stream of future cash flows under uncertainty?

The withdrawal rate is not a preference knob

Choosing 5% because it produces a smaller target does not make that target equally resilient. The rate is an assumption about how aggressively the portfolio will be asked to fund spending. Longer horizons, rigid spending and poor early returns generally require more caution; flexible spending or later guaranteed income can change the picture.

Early financial independence can mean a 40–50 year liability

A person reaching FI at 45 may need the portfolio to support far more years than a traditional retiree at 65. Morningstar’s September 2026 early-retirement research illustrates how its model-based starting rate falls as the horizon lengthens. This does not create a universal FI rate, but it highlights why blindly importing a 30-year retirement rule into a 50-year FI plan is risky.

Do not hide big one-off goals inside a withdrawal rate

A home purchase, university support, major travel, business capital or family gift may not be part of recurring living expenses. Model these as separate future liabilities. Otherwise the FI number can look adequate for annual spending while being underfunded once a large goal arrives.

Housing needs explicit treatment

  • If the home is paid off and you plan to remain there, ongoing housing costs still include maintenance, taxes/fees, insurance and utilities.
  • If you rent, include rent inflation and relocation risk.
  • If you plan to downsize or unlock home equity, model the timing, transaction costs and realistic net proceeds rather than counting the full property value as spendable today.

Your FI number is a moving target until expressed consistently

If the target is calculated in today’s money, update it as current spending changes. If it is a future nominal number, inflate the spending to the expected FI date. Mixing today’s expenses with a future portfolio value can create a misleading gap.

The FI number should include the cost of not having an employer

Leaving paid employment can change health coverage, insurance, retirement-plan subsidies, commuting costs, taxes and professional expenses. Some costs disappear while others become personally funded. Build the FI lifestyle from the post-work system, not simply from last year’s bank statement.

Taxes can make a pre-tax FI number misleading

If ₹12 lakh of annual spending requires ₹14 lakh of gross withdrawals after tax, the portfolio-funded liability is closer to the gross number. Tax depends on account type and jurisdiction, so the correct approach is to model after-tax spending and a realistic gross withdrawal path rather than add a universal percentage.

Partial FI can reduce fragility

A household that can cover half its expenses from optional work needs a smaller portfolio withdrawal and may be able to tolerate a market shock better. This does not redefine full financial independence, but it shows why FI can be viewed as a spectrum of dependence on earned income rather than a single binary day.

Frequently asked questions

Is 25× annual expenses the FI number?

It is a first-pass estimate based on a 4% starting withdrawal. It is not a universal guarantee, especially for very long horizons or inflexible spending.

Should I include my primary home?

Only if you have a realistic plan to convert part of its value into cash flow. Otherwise count the ongoing housing costs and treat the home separately from the spendable portfolio.

Do I subtract pension or Social Security-type income?

If the income is reasonably dependable, model it by start date and after-tax amount. It can reduce the portfolio-funded spending after it begins.

How often should I update the FI number?

Whenever spending, family structure, housing, major goals, retirement horizon or reliable income changes materially; an annual review is a practical minimum for a long-term plan.
