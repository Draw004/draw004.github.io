What is longevity risk and why does retirement length matter?

Longevity risk is the financial risk of needing income and spending support for longer than the plan assumed.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide

Direct answer

Longevity risk is the possibility that you live longer than the period your financial plan was built to fund. It is not a prediction that you will live to a specific age; it is a planning problem created by uncertainty around lifespan, healthcare and the number of years over which assets must support spending.

A longer life is good news, but it extends the financial horizon.

If a retirement plan is built only through age 80 and spending continues to age 95, the missing 15 years can be material. NISM identifies longevity risk as one of the major retirement risks alongside sequence-of-returns and inflation risk.

Longevity changes more than the number of withdrawals

  • Inflation has more time to raise nominal spending.
  • Healthcare and support costs may change later in life.
  • The portfolio has less room to recover from weak early returns.
  • A fixed withdrawal shortcut may be stretched beyond the horizon for which it was originally studied.

Retirement age and plan-until age are different inputs

Retirement age answers when earned income may reduce or stop. Plan-until age answers how long the portfolio should be tested. Moving retirement earlier usually shortens the accumulation period and lengthens the drawdown period at the same time.

Use scenarios instead of guessing one exact lifespan

No calculator can know an individual lifespan. A practical approach is to test more than one plan-until age and see how sensitive the result is. If the plan only works with a short horizon, that dependence is important information.

Longevity interacts with other income sources

Pensions, annuity income, rental income or other dependable cash flows can reduce the amount that must be withdrawn from investments. Their timing, inflation behavior and reliability should be modelled separately rather than simply deducted without context.

Average life expectancy is not a safe end date for an individual plan

Life expectancy is an average across a population with age, sex, health and other assumptions. Roughly half of a comparable population can live beyond an average. Couples also face “last survivor” risk: the household plan may need to continue until the second person dies, not the first.

Longevity risk compounds other retirement risks

A longer retirement creates more years of inflation, more market sequences, more healthcare uncertainty and more time for spending assumptions to drift. CFA Institute’s 2026 material explicitly connects longevity risk with sequence-of-return risk, while OECD research emphasizes that future mortality improvements need to be considered in retirement-income provision.

A longer horizon changes withdrawal math

The same portfolio must support more withdrawals and endure more uncertainty. That is why early-retirement research generally shows lower sustainable starting withdrawal rates for 40- or 50-year horizons than for 30 years. Simply assuming that additional equity exposure will solve the extra years can increase sequence risk.

Lifetime income and portfolio assets solve different parts of the problem

Pensions and annuity-like income can transfer some longevity risk because payments may continue for life, subject to provider, inflation and contract terms. A liquid investment portfolio provides flexibility, growth potential and legacy value but leaves more longevity risk with the household. Many plans combine both rather than treating them as substitutes.

Spending may not decline smoothly with age

Some households spend more on travel and activities early in retirement, less in the middle years and more again if healthcare or care needs rise. CFA Institute has described this type of “horseshoe” pattern. A plan that assumes a constant real spending line should therefore be stress-tested for late-life costs.

Practical longevity defenses

  • Avoid setting the plan’s end date equal to a population average without a stress case.
  • Protect essential spending with stable/lifetime income where appropriate and available.
  • Maintain diversified growth assets for a long horizon instead of de-risking the entire portfolio too early.
  • Keep liquidity for healthcare, home adaptation and other late-life shocks.
  • Use flexible withdrawals for discretionary spending rather than treating every rupee as permanently fixed.
  • Review beneficiary, caregiving and survivor-income implications for couples.

Longevity also creates operational and cognitive planning risks

A very long retirement may include periods when managing a complex portfolio becomes difficult. Simplifying accounts, documenting the plan, maintaining trusted contacts and planning powers of attorney or equivalent local arrangements can be part of financial resilience. These legal tools differ by jurisdiction, so local advice may be needed.

The survivor’s balance sheet can change abruptly

One death can reduce pension income, alter taxes, trigger housing decisions and leave a less financially engaged partner responsible for the portfolio. A longevity plan should therefore test both “both alive” and “one survivor” cash flows, not just extend the same household budget to age 100.

Late-life liquidity can be more valuable than maximum return

Home adaptation, care deposits, medical travel or family support can require cash quickly. A plan invested solely for long-run expected return may be awkward if large illiquid assets dominate precisely when care decisions become urgent.

Frequently asked questions

What age should I plan to?

Use a range and include a long-life stress scenario. The appropriate age depends on personal circumstances and local mortality data; a single average is not a guarantee.

Is living longer always financially bad?

No. Longevity is desirable; the risk is only that resources are not structured to support the additional years.

Do annuities eliminate longevity risk?

Certain lifetime-income products can transfer part of the risk, but inflation protection, provider risk, liquidity, fees and contract terms matter. Product availability differs by country.

Why is longevity especially important for couples?

The household may need assets and income to last until the longer-lived partner dies, and some pensions or benefits can fall after the first death while many household expenses remain.
