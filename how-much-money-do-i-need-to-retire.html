RETIREMENT PLANNING GUIDE

How much money do you need to retire?

A useful retirement number starts with the life you expect to fund, not with a round target picked from a headline.

BEFORE YOU START

Start with spending, then model how long the money may need to last.

There is no single retirement corpus that is right for everyone. Two people with the same salary can need very different amounts because their spending, retirement age, housing costs, healthcare needs, pensions, family commitments and desired safety margin may be different.

A practical retirement estimate usually has four stages: estimate retirement spending in today's money, grow those costs for inflation until retirement, model the years after retirement, and then compare the required corpus with the assets and income you expect to have.

A simple retirement planning sequence1. Estimate the lifestyle you want to fund in today's money.2. Allow for inflation between now and retirement.3. Include retirement income such as pensions or other dependable cash flows.4. Model how long the remaining portfolio may need to support withdrawals.5. Compare the target with existing savings and future contributions.

Why salary is not the best starting point

Salary is useful for understanding how much you may be able to save, but retirement is funded by expenses. If you earn a high income but live on a modest portion of it, a salary multiple can overstate your needs. If you spend most of your income, support family members or expect large healthcare and housing costs, the same salary multiple can understate them.

For planning purposes, separate expenses into categories. Housing may fall after a mortgage ends. Commuting costs may disappear. Travel may rise. Healthcare may rise later in retirement. Some costs continue for life while others are temporary.

Inflation can change the number dramatically

Suppose a household spends 100 units per month today. At a constant 5% annual inflation assumption, the same basket would cost about 163 units in 10 years, 265 units in 20 years and 432 units in 30 years. That is not a forecast; it is a scenario showing why today's spending and future spending should not be mixed.

Planning principle: use today's money to describe the lifestyle, then let the model convert it into future money. That keeps the assumptions visible.

Retirement length matters as much as retirement age

Retiring at 55 rather than 65 has two effects at the same time: there are fewer years to build the portfolio and more years in which the portfolio may need to fund spending. Planning to age 90 rather than 80 also changes the required amount. A retirement calculator should therefore ask both when retirement begins and how long the plan should run.

What about the 25x rule?

A common shortcut is to multiply annual retirement spending by 25, which is mathematically the same as dividing spending by a 4% first-year withdrawal rate. It can be a useful sense check, but it is not a personalized retirement plan. Taxes, inflation, investment returns, retirement length, sequence of returns, pensions and one-time goals can all move the answer materially.

Existing savings should reduce the gap, not disappear from the calculation

What you have already saved has time to grow before retirement. A good model projects existing savings separately from new contributions, then shows the remaining gap. That is more informative than calculating a target corpus in isolation.

Use a range, not one magical number

Try more than one inflation rate, return assumption and retirement age. If a plan only works under the most optimistic inputs, that is useful information. If it remains reasonably funded across a range of assumptions, that is also useful information.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a universal retirement corpus?

No. A useful estimate depends on spending, retirement age, years in retirement, inflation, investment returns, retirement income and existing savings. A round number can be a checkpoint, but it should not replace a cash-flow model.

Should I use salary or expenses to plan retirement?

Salary helps estimate saving capacity. Expenses are usually more directly connected to the amount a retirement portfolio must fund. A spending-based model also makes lifestyle assumptions easier to see.

How often should I recalculate?

Revisit the plan after meaningful changes in spending, savings, retirement age, pension expectations or major goals. An annual review is a reasonable planning habit for many households, but the right frequency depends on how much has changed.
