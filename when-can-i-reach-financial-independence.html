FINANCIAL INDEPENDENCE GUIDE

When could you reach financial independence?

The timeline depends on spending, current assets, monthly contributions, return assumptions and the target multiple you choose.

CarrowmontUpdated 9 October 2026Educational planning guide

Direct answer

You reach financial independence when the resources you have are sufficient—under your chosen assumptions—to fund the lifestyle and goals you define without relying on ongoing earned income. The date depends on both sides of the equation: how fast your assets grow and how large the FI target becomes.

BEFORE YOU START

Financial independence is a target plus a path.

First estimate the portfolio needed to support the spending level you want to fund. Then compare that target with existing assets and the projected value of future contributions.

Spending has a double effect.

Lower ongoing spending can reduce the target and may also leave more current cash flow available for contributions. Higher spending does the opposite.

The withdrawal assumption changes the target.

A 4% spending-to-portfolio ratio implies 25 times annual spending; 3.5% implies about 28.6 times. These are modelling assumptions, not guarantees of portfolio longevity.

Existing assets matter because they already have time to grow.

The same monthly contribution can lead to a very different timeline depending on whether the starting portfolio is near zero or already substantial.

FI is a race between the portfolio and the target

Most calculators project investment assets forward, but the target can also move. If lifestyle spending rises, housing changes or inflation is higher than expected, the required portfolio increases. A strong FI model therefore projects both the asset path and the spending-based target path.

FI crossing conditionProjected investable assets ≥ FI target under the same money basis and withdrawal assumption

The savings rate is powerful because it affects both sides

Saving more increases contributions while often reducing the lifestyle spending that the future portfolio must support. That double effect is why the percentage of income saved can be more informative for FI timing than salary alone.

Use real-return math when working in today’s money

If the FI target is expressed in today’s purchasing power, project the portfolio using a real return after inflation, or convert the target to future nominal money. Do not grow assets at a nominal 8% while holding the target permanently in today’s purchasing power unless inflation has been handled elsewhere.

A date is less useful than a range

Instead of “FI at 51 years 4 months,” test conservative, base and favorable scenarios. Return uncertainty alone can move the crossing date by years, and life changes can move it further. A date range communicates uncertainty more honestly and creates decision points rather than false precision.

Near FI, sequence and valuation risk start to matter more

A person can cross the target after a strong market rally and fall back below it after a correction. Before reducing earned income permanently, test whether the plan survives an immediate bear market, several years of high inflation and a longer-than-expected horizon.

Define what “independent” means operationally

  • Will you stop work completely, or keep optional income?
  • Are taxes and health insurance included in the spending target?
  • Are children/parents or other family commitments included?
  • Are one-off goals funded separately?
  • What spending could be reduced if markets disappoint?
  • What portfolio liquidity is available for the first years after earned income falls?

The “one more year” effect can be powerful near the finish line

Working one additional year near FI can have three effects at once: one more year of contributions, one less year of withdrawals, and another year for the existing portfolio to grow. The effect is not guaranteed because markets can fall, but it is often much larger than one year divided by the remaining gap.

Crossing the line after a bull market deserves a stress test

If the FI date arrives because assets surged 25% in one year, immediately test a significant drawdown and the resulting withdrawal rate. A target should represent durable funding capacity, not just a market snapshot.

Use milestones before full FI

Emergency independence, debt freedom, one-income resilience, Coast FI and partial FI can create meaningful options before the full target is reached. Tracking those milestones can make a multi-decade journey useful rather than treating every year before full FI as failure.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my FI date change when I change inflation?

Inflation changes the future cost of the lifestyle the portfolio must support. If you use real returns and today’s-money spending consistently, the effect is captured in the real return.

Can I use an optimistic return to reach FI sooner on paper?

You can model it as an upside scenario, but it should not be the only plan. A higher assumption does not create the return; it usually creates a more fragile projected date.

What if I plan to earn a little after FI?

Model the income explicitly and conservatively. Reliable part-time income can reduce portfolio withdrawals, but optional work should not be counted as guaranteed if your definition of FI requires the ability to stop.

Should I wait until I am far above the FI number?

That is a personal margin decision. A useful approach is to stress-test the plan rather than rely on an arbitrary extra percentage.
