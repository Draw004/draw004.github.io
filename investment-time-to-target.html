How long could it take to reach your investment target?

The time to a target depends on your starting balance, monthly contribution, return assumption and whether contributions increase over time.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Estimate your time to target→

Direct answer

Time to target is a moving result, not a fixed promise. It depends on the starting balance, ongoing contributions, assumed return and whether the target itself grows with inflation. A useful calculation solves for the first period in which the projected balance reaches the stated future target.

BEFORE YOU START

Time to target is the reverse of a future-value calculation.

Instead of asking what a monthly contribution may become after a fixed period, set the target first and find the first modelled month in which the projected balance reaches it.

Existing savings can shorten the path.

A starting balance has the entire horizon to compound. When it is included separately from new monthly contributions, the model shows how much of the target is being funded by money already invested.

A higher return assumption shortens the modelled time, but it is not a promise.

Return assumptions are scenarios. A plan that only works under a high return may be more fragile than one that still works under a lower assumption.

Increasing contributions changes the path too.

If the monthly amount rises over time, the target may be reached sooner, but the later contribution commitment also becomes larger.

Why time-to-target is harder than a single future-value calculation

When the monthly contribution is known, you can project the balance forward period by period until it crosses the target. If the target is inflation-linked, both sides of the equation move: the portfolio may grow, but the amount required can also rise.

Target test each monthProjected balance(t) ≥ Required target(t)For an inflation-linked goal, Required target(t) may itself equal today’s goal cost × (1+inflation)^t.

The table is not a recommendation to chase a target faster. It shows the mechanics: if the date matters, contribution and time are transparent levers. Raising the assumed return can also shorten the modelled date, but it does not make the higher return more likely.

A fixed date and a flexible date lead to different decisions

For a fixed-date goal—such as tuition due in a particular year—the contribution may need to adjust because the date cannot. For a flexible goal, such as optional early retirement, the date can move with market outcomes. The planning tool should reflect which variable is truly negotiable.

Approaching the target changes the risk problem

When the goal is far away, short-term volatility may be tolerable if the asset mix is appropriate. As the goal approaches, a large market decline can be harder to recover from before the money is needed. Many plans therefore gradually increase the share of assets chosen for capital stability or near-term liquidity as the funding date gets closer.

Use milestone reviews instead of watching the date every day

  • Review after material contribution changes, major market moves or changes to the goal—not after every normal fluctuation.
  • Track funded percentage as well as the estimated date. A 70% funded goal conveys more context than “3.8 years remaining.”
  • Separate nominal progress from inflation-adjusted progress for long-dated goals.
  • If the projected date keeps slipping, identify whether the cause is lower contributions, lower returns or a rising target before changing the investment risk.

A target date is the output of several assumptions, not a countdown clock

When a calculator says a target may be reached in 11.4 years, that precision comes from a smooth assumed return. Markets do not deliver the same return every month, so the real crossing date can arrive earlier, later, or more than once. A better interpretation is a range of plausible dates under different return and contribution scenarios.

Hard-date and soft-date goals should be modeled differently

A university fee due in 2038 has a hard liability date. Early retirement may be flexible. For a hard-date goal, contribution and risk should adapt because the date cannot. For a soft-date goal, the date itself can absorb some uncertainty.

Track the funded ratio as the date gets closer

Once most of the goal is funded, protecting the accumulated capital can matter more than maximizing expected return. A 25% market decline when the goal is 90% funded can add years to the recovery path. The appropriate risk level therefore depends not only on years remaining but also on how much of the liability is already funded.

  • Report a conservative/base/favorable date range.
  • Reduce false precision to whole months or years where appropriate.
  • For fixed dates, define a glide path for money that soon must be spent.

Frequently asked questions

Can a calculator predict the exact month I will reach a target?

No. It can identify the month under a stated return path. Actual market returns and contributions vary, so the real date can be earlier or later.

What if the target is in today’s money?

Inflate the target to the future date, or compare the portfolio in real terms. Otherwise you may “reach” a nominal number that no longer buys what you intended.

Should I increase risk if I am behind schedule?

Higher risk can increase expected return but also the chance of a loss, especially near a fixed goal date. First examine contribution, time and goal flexibility before assuming more risk is the solution.
