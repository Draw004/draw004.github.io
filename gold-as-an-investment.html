Gold as an investment: what role can it play in a financial plan?

Gold can behave differently from stocks, bonds and property, but it is still an investment with price risk rather than a guaranteed store of value.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide

Gold is often discussed alongside inflation. Use the Inflation Calculator to separate the concept of rising prices from assumptions about any one asset.

Direct answer

Gold can act as a diversifier and liquid store of value in some portfolios, but it is not a bond, a business or a guaranteed safe haven. It produces no operating cash flow, its price can be volatile, and the investment case depends on how it interacts with the rest of the portfolio rather than on a belief that gold must always rise during inflation or crisis.

Gold is an asset, but it does not behave like a business or a bond.

A share can represent ownership in a business that may produce earnings and dividends. A bond represents lending to an issuer and may pay contractual interest. Gold does not generate operating earnings or a coupon. Its investment return comes mainly from changes in its market price, less any costs of buying, holding, storing or accessing it.

Why do investors include gold?

The World Gold Council describes gold as a liquid asset that may contribute diversification because its return pattern can differ from equities and other risk assets. Diversification can reduce dependence on a single return driver, but it cannot eliminate losses.

The form of gold matters.

Exposure can come through physical bullion, exchange-traded products and other locally available structures. Each has different storage, custody, transaction-cost, tracking, regulatory and tax characteristics. A product that follows the gold price is not operationally identical to holding physical metal.

Gold can diversify a portfolio without being the whole portfolio.

A useful planning question is not “Will gold go up?” but “What job is this asset meant to perform?” A portfolio built around one asset class can become concentrated even when that asset has recently performed well.

  • Define whether the purpose is diversification, liquidity, long-term wealth storage or a specific future purchase.
  • Compare fees, spreads, storage and tax treatment in your country.
  • Avoid treating recent price performance as a forecast.
  • Consider how gold interacts with your equity, fixed-income, cash and property exposure.

Start with what gold is—and what it is not

A share represents ownership in a business that can generate earnings and dividends. A bond is a contractual claim on an issuer that promises interest and principal subject to credit risk. Gold is a scarce physical asset whose investment return comes primarily from changes in market price, less ownership and transaction costs. That difference changes how you should think about valuation and expected return.

Gold has several demand engines

Gold demand comes from investment, central banks, jewellery and technology. Because these buyers respond to different economic conditions, gold does not behave exactly like a typical industrial commodity. Interest rates, real yields, currency movements, inflation expectations, financial stress and investor positioning can all influence the price. No single variable explains every period.

Diversification is about correlation, not whether an asset “always goes up”

A useful diversifier does not need to outperform stocks. Its value can come from behaving differently when the rest of the portfolio is under stress. World Gold Council research argues that gold’s correlation with risk assets has historically become more defensive during some equity sell-offs. That is industry research, not a guarantee that the relationship will repeat in every crisis.

Important nuance

Gold can fall at the same time as stocks, especially over short windows. “Safe haven” should never be read as “cannot lose money.” Regulators such as the CFTC explicitly warn that precious-metal prices can be volatile and that high-pressure sales claims about guaranteed safety are misleading.

Inflation protection is more complicated than the slogan

Gold has preserved purchasing power over some long historical periods and has performed strongly in some high-inflation episodes. But its short-term relationship with consumer inflation is unstable. A one-year inflation spike does not mechanically produce a one-year gold gain. Interest rates, currency and investor expectations can dominate in the short run.

Currency matters for non-US investors

Global gold is commonly referenced in US dollars, but an investor experiences returns in their home currency. If the local currency weakens against the dollar, local gold prices can rise even if the USD gold price is flat; the reverse can also occur. That means a local investor’s gold return combines the underlying gold-price move with currency effects.

How to judge whether gold adds something to a portfolio

  1. Identify the existing risks: equity concentration, currency exposure, liquidity needs and inflation sensitivity.
  2. Decide what role gold is supposed to play—diversification, liquidity, inflation uncertainty or speculation.
  3. Compare the chosen vehicle’s costs, custody and tax treatment in your jurisdiction.
  4. Stress-test the portfolio with gold both rising and falling; do not evaluate only the scenario that supports the allocation.
  5. Set a rebalancing policy so a large gold rally does not silently turn a small diversifier into a dominant position.

What gold cannot solve

  • It does not create an emergency fund if the holding is volatile or difficult to sell quickly.
  • It does not eliminate the need for productive growth assets in a long-term plan.
  • It does not guarantee protection against every inflation episode or market crash.
  • It does not become low risk merely because it is physical. Dealer spreads, fraud, storage and concentration can matter.
  • It is not a substitute for understanding the overall asset allocation.

A deeper valuation problem: gold has no internal cash-flow anchor

A stock can be analyzed through earnings and cash flow; a bond has contractual payments. Gold produces neither. Its value is therefore heavily influenced by what investors are willing to pay for scarcity, liquidity, diversification, currency protection and perceived safety. That makes “fair value” less observable and helps explain why long periods of strong or weak performance can occur without a change in operating profit—because there is no operating business.

Carry and opportunity cost matter

Physical gold may have storage, insurance and dealing spreads. A fund may have management fees and tracking difference. The opportunity cost of holding gold also changes with real interest rates: when safe assets offer attractive inflation-adjusted yields, a non-yielding asset can face a different competitive environment than when real yields are low or negative.

Judge gold by portfolio contribution, not isolated return

The useful question is not “Did gold beat stocks this decade?” but “Did a small allocation improve the portfolio’s behavior across the scenarios that matter?” Compare volatility, drawdowns, correlation and rebalancing value as well as return. A diversifier can add value even when it is not the highest-return asset.

Frequently asked questions

Does gold generate income?

Gold itself does not pay a coupon or dividend. Returns depend mainly on price changes, while some gold vehicles may have fees that reduce the investor’s net result.

Is gold guaranteed to rise when inflation rises?

No. The long-run store-of-value argument is different from a short-run one-to-one inflation hedge. Gold can fall during periods of positive inflation.

Is physical gold safer than a gold ETF?

They have different risks. Physical ownership adds storage, authenticity, spread and insurance considerations. An exchange-traded vehicle adds fund structure, fee, market-price and tracking considerations. “Safer” depends on which risk matters to you.

How much gold should a portfolio hold?

There is no universal percentage. The appropriate role depends on the rest of the portfolio, risk tolerance, liquidity, time horizon and local products. Carrowmont should explain the trade-offs rather than prescribe an allocation.
