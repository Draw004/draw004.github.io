What is rental yield, and how do you calculate it?

Rental yield measures income from a property relative to its value or purchase cost; it is not the same thing as total investment return.

If a property purchase requires a deposit or future lump sum, model that funding target separately from assumptions about rental income.

Gross rental yield is a simple first-pass measure.

Gross rental yield = annual rent ÷ property value × 100
IllustrationProperty value: 300,000 currency unitsAnnual rent: 15,000Gross rental yield: 5%

Net yield is closer to the income actually retained.

Gross yield ignores many ownership costs. Net yield can account for maintenance, property management, insurance, taxes, service charges, vacancy and other recurring expenses. Definitions vary, so state which costs are included before comparing properties.

Net rental yield = (annual rent − recurring property expenses) ÷ capital basis × 100

Financing changes cash flow but not the property’s underlying gross yield.

A mortgage can reduce the equity capital initially required, but interest and principal payments affect the owner’s cash flow. Leverage can amplify gains and losses in the owner’s equity and should be analyzed separately from the property’s unlevered rental yield.

Yield and capital appreciation are different return sources.

A low-yield property can still rise in value, while a high-yield property can fall in value. Neither outcome is guaranteed. Combining the two without separating assumptions makes comparisons less transparent.

Frequently asked questions

What is a “good” rental yield?

There is no universal number. Yields differ by country, city, property type, interest rates, taxes, maintenance and growth expectations. A higher yield can also reflect higher risk or lower expected price growth.

Should vacancy be included?

For a realistic net-income estimate, expected vacancy and collection risk should be considered rather than assuming full occupancy every month.

Does rental yield include mortgage payments?

Gross yield does not. Net yield definitions vary. Financing is usually best shown as a separate cash-flow layer so you can distinguish property economics from your financing structure.
