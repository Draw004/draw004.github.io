What is rental yield, and how do you calculate it?

Rental yield measures income from a property relative to its value or purchase cost; it is not the same thing as total investment return.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide

If a property purchase requires a deposit or future lump sum, model that funding target separately from assumptions about rental income.

Direct answer

Rental yield measures rental income relative to property value, but gross yield is only a first screen. The economically useful number is closer to net operating yield after vacancy, management, maintenance, property charges, insurance and other recurring costs. Financing then determines the return on the investor’s equity.

Gross rental yield is a simple first-pass measure.

Gross rental yield = annual rent ÷ property value × 100
IllustrationProperty value: 300,000 currency unitsAnnual rent: 15,000Gross rental yield: 5%

Net yield is closer to the income actually retained.

Gross yield ignores many ownership costs. Net yield can account for maintenance, property management, insurance, taxes, service charges, vacancy and other recurring expenses. Definitions vary, so state which costs are included before comparing properties.

Net rental yield = (annual rent − recurring property expenses) ÷ capital basis × 100

Financing changes cash flow but not the property’s underlying gross yield.

A mortgage can reduce the equity capital initially required, but interest and principal payments affect the owner’s cash flow. Leverage can amplify gains and losses in the owner’s equity and should be analyzed separately from the property’s unlevered rental yield.

Yield and capital appreciation are different return sources.

A low-yield property can still rise in value, while a high-yield property can fall in value. Neither outcome is guaranteed. Combining the two without separating assumptions makes comparisons less transparent.

Gross rental yieldAnnual gross rent ÷ Property purchase/value × 100%
Net operating yield(Annual rent − vacancy − operating expenses) ÷ Property value × 100%Financing costs are often analyzed separately so the underlying property economics can be compared across different loan structures.

Gross yield can be dangerously flattering

A property renting for 6 units per year on a 100-unit value has a 6% gross yield. If vacancy, maintenance, management, insurance and property charges consume 2 units, the operating yield is closer to 4% before financing and tax. A buyer who compares the 6% headline yield with a bond yield or savings rate is not comparing like with like.

Leverage changes cash-on-cash return

If the property is financed, the investor may contribute only part of the purchase price as equity. Rental income after operating costs then has to cover interest and principal payments. A small change in rent, vacancy or interest cost can create a much larger percentage change in the return on the investor’s equity.

Illustrative leverage effectProperty value: 100 units; investor equity: 25; debt: 75.Net operating income before financing: 4 units.If annual interest/financing cash cost is 3.5 units, pre-tax cash flow is only 0.5 units before principal effects.A 1-unit decline in net operating income can turn positive cash flow negative even though the gross rental yield may still look attractive.

Yield is not total return

Total property return can include rental income plus changes in the property’s market value, less transaction and capital costs. A low-yield property can still produce a strong total return if appreciation is high; a high-yield property can disappoint if vacancy, repairs or falling prices dominate. Neither outcome is guaranteed.

Use yield to ask better due-diligence questions

  • Is the quoted rent actual, current and sustainable?
  • How many months of vacancy are realistic over a full cycle?
  • Which costs are paid by the owner versus tenant?
  • What large maintenance items are likely within the holding period?
  • How liquid is the local property market if you need to sell?
  • What return would the same equity need to earn elsewhere to be competitive after adjusting for risk and effort?

Gross yield is an advertisement; net yield is the economic starting point

Purchase price and rent are easy to observe, so gross yield is common. But vacancy, management, maintenance, insurance, property taxes/fees and recurring capital expenditure determine how much cash the property actually produces. A property with a high gross yield can have a mediocre net yield if costs and vacancy are high.

Net rental yield(Annual rent − recurring operating costs) ÷ property value

Cash-on-cash return is different again

When debt is used, investors often compare annual pre-tax cash flow with the actual equity invested. This can look much higher or lower than the property yield because financing changes both cash flow and risk. Always show debt service and leverage separately rather than implying the property itself earned the leveraged return.

Stress vacancy and major repairs

A one-month vacancy removes roughly 8.3% of annual gross rent before any repair or letting costs. A major roof, appliance or structural expense can consume several years of net income. Reserve for these events when judging whether rental income is truly attractive.

Frequently asked questions

What is a “good” rental yield?

There is no universal number. A higher yield may compensate for higher vacancy, maintenance, location or tenant risk. Compare net yield, financing, expected capital costs and local alternatives.

Should mortgage interest be deducted in net rental yield?

Analysts often separate property operating yield from financing so the asset can be compared independently of loan structure. For personal cash flow, financing absolutely matters and should be included in a cash-on-cash analysis.

Does gross yield include property appreciation?

No. Yield measures income relative to property value. Appreciation is a separate component of total return.

Why can two identical-looking properties have different net yields?

Taxes, association fees, management, maintenance needs, vacancy and purchase price can differ even if headline rent is similar.
