Rent vs buy: how should you compare the financial trade-offs?

Renting and buying are not simply “money lost” versus “money invested”; they are different bundles of costs, risks, flexibility and ownership.

CarrowmontUpdated 27 September 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO USE YOUR OWN NUMBERS?Plan a home down-payment goal→

If buying is one of your goals, use the Goal Planner to separate the down-payment target and timeline from assumptions about future property prices.

Start with how long you expect to stay.

Buying and selling property usually involves transaction costs. The CFPB notes that buying can make more sense when you expect to remain in the home long enough for those costs to be worthwhile. A short or uncertain time horizon increases the value of renting flexibility.

Compare the full cost of ownership, not only the mortgage payment.

Ownership can include mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, insurance, association charges, repairs, maintenance, transaction costs and opportunity cost on the down payment. Renting can include rent increases, deposits, moving costs and less control over the property.

FactorRentingBuying
Up-front capitalUsually lowerDown payment and transaction/closing costs can be substantial
MobilityTypically easier to relocateSelling can take time and cost money
MaintenanceOften landlord responsibility subject to lease/local lawOwner bears repair and maintenance costs
Price exposureNo direct home-price equityValue can rise or fall; leverage amplifies exposure
Payment structureRent can change over timeMortgage structure may be fixed or variable depending on market

The down payment has an opportunity cost.

Money used as a down payment cannot simultaneously remain in cash or another investment. A fair comparison should acknowledge what that capital might otherwise do, while also recognizing that alternative investment returns are uncertain.

Scenario analysis is better than a single break-even answer.

CFPB guidance specifically warns that rent-versus-buy calculators depend on assumptions such as home-price growth. Test several scenarios for appreciation, rent growth, financing cost, maintenance and holding period instead of treating one outcome as certain.

Frequently asked questions

Is rent always “wasted money”?

No. Rent pays for housing services and flexibility. Buying also has costs that do not build equity, including interest, taxes, insurance, repairs and transaction fees.

Does buying always build wealth?

Not necessarily. Property values can decline, transaction costs can be high, and leverage can magnify both gains and losses.

Should tax benefits decide the answer?

Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction and household. Country-specific rules should be checked separately rather than built into a universal rent-versus-buy rule.
