Learn before you decide

Understand the ideas behind the numbers.

Financial planning is easier to use when you understand what each assumption means. These guides explain core concepts in plain language and connect them directly to Carrowmont's tools.

01
FOUNDATION

Today's money and future money are not the same thing

Today's money describes purchasing power in terms you recognize now. Future money is the nominal currency amount that may be needed at a later date after applying an inflation assumption.

If something costs 100 today, a future price of 200 does not automatically mean the lifestyle has doubled. Part of the difference may simply reflect a lower purchasing power of each currency unit.

Why Carrowmont shows both: comparing today's purchasing power with a future nominal amount helps prevent two different concepts from being mistaken for the same number.

02
INFLATION

Inflation changes future costs and purchasing power

Inflation describes a general rise in prices over time. If prices increase while the amount of money you hold stays unchanged, that money buys less than it did before.

Carrowmont's inflation calculations use a rate you enter and compound it over the selected time horizon. The result is an illustration, not a forecast: real inflation changes from year to year and households can experience different cost increases.

Two useful questions:What might today's expense cost in future currency?What might the same nominal amount be able to buy in today's purchasing power?

03
RETIREMENT

Your retirement budget does not have to be today's budget with inflation added

Some costs may disappear before retirement, such as school fees, a mortgage or commuting. Other costs may continue, while healthcare, travel or domestic support may change. Retirement income such as a pension or rent can also reduce how much must come from investments.

A more useful retirement model therefore asks which expenses remain, when they change, and how they inflate rather than applying one assumption to every part of today's household spending.

Keep the distinction clear: a retirement corpus is an estimate produced by assumptions about spending, income, inflation, returns and time. It is not a guarantee that a portfolio will last.

04
SAVING

Compounding is the interaction of time, contributions and return assumptions

When an amount earns a return and that return remains invested, future returns can apply to both the original amount and earlier gains. Over long periods, time can therefore have a large effect on an illustrative future value.

The same mechanism also works in reverse when projecting future prices: repeated inflation compounds on prior price increases. Small changes in a long-term rate assumption can therefore lead to materially different outcomes.

Use return assumptions carefully: investment returns are not constant in real life. A planning rate is a modelling input, not a promise of future performance.
05
RESILIENCE

Short-term financial resilience is different from long-term investing

Money needed for unexpected expenses or near-term obligations has a different job from money intended for a long retirement or distant goal. Liquidity, timing and the possibility of needing the money quickly can matter more than maximizing a projected long-term return.

There is no single emergency-fund amount that fits every household. Income stability, essential expenses, insurance, dependants, access to credit and personal circumstances all affect the amount of readily available cash someone may prefer to keep.

Planning principle: separate money by purpose before comparing long-term return assumptions.
06
LIFE GOALS

Turn a future goal into four clearer questions

Whether the goal is education, a home, travel, a career change or another milestone, the calculation becomes easier to understand when you separate the assumptions.

  1. What would the goal cost today?Start with a value you can understand in current purchasing power.
  2. When might the money be needed?The time horizon affects both inflation and the time available to save.
  3. How might that cost change?Different goals can experience different price growth.
  4. What resources are already available?Existing savings and future contributions change the remaining gap.

Carrowmont's Goal Planner is planned as a future tool. Until then, these questions provide a useful framework for thinking about a goal without pretending the future is certain.
