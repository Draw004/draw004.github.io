What is an index fund, and what does it actually track?

Follow the index rules, costs and concentration — not only the word “passive.”

By CarrowmontPublished and reviewed 20 October 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy

30-second answer

An index fund is a pooled investment designed to follow a defined market index rather than asking a manager to choose every holding. It may be organised as a mutual fund or an ETF. Its value still rises and falls with the assets in the index, and its actual result can differ from the index after fees, taxes, trading and tracking effects.

Fund structures, tax rules and the availability of automatic investment plans differ by country. The core idea — following a defined index — is global.

What is a market index?

An index is a set of rules used to measure a selected part of a market. It might represent large companies in one country, global companies, government bonds, corporate bonds, small companies or a narrow sector. The index itself is a calculation. You normally cannot invest directly in the number.

An index provider decides the eligibility rules, weighting method, review schedule and how companies or bonds enter and leave. Two indexes that sound similar may produce different portfolios.

What does an index fund do?

The fund aims to produce a result close to its chosen index before or after stated costs, depending on how performance is reported. It may hold every security in the index or a representative sample. Some funds use derivatives as part of the approach.

The aim is usually to track, not to beat, the index. That makes the method easier to explain, but it does not make the outcome certain.

An index fund can be a mutual fund or an ETF

Index fund describes the investment strategy. ETF describes an exchange-traded structure. Many ETFs are index funds, but some ETFs are actively managed. Some mutual funds also follow indexes.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: Asking whether an ETF or an index fund is better as though they are always different categories. The real comparison may be an index ETF versus an index mutual fund that follows the same or a similar benchmark.

Worked example: The index rule creates concentration

Imagine an index with five companies. Under a market-cap weighting rule, the weights are 40%, 25%, 15%, 10% and 10%. A fund that follows the index closely will also place about 65% in the two largest companies.

The example shows why a fund with several holdings can still be concentrated. The number of securities is useful, but weights matter too.

Passive does not mean risk-free

An index fund follows the market segment through rises and falls. It does not usually move to cash because prices look expensive or because a manager expects a recession. If the index falls sharply, a fund tracking it can fall sharply too.

Other risks include concentration, interest-rate risk in bond indexes, currency risk in foreign holdings, liquidity risk, index-method changes and the possibility that the chosen market segment does not fit the goal.

Fees, tracking difference and tracking error

Fees reduce what remains for investors. Tracking difference is the gap between the fund’s return and the index return over a period. Tracking error describes how variable that gap is over time.

A fund may lag because of fees, taxes, transaction costs, cash holdings, sampling, index changes or trading timing. A fund can also temporarily outperform the index for technical reasons; that does not change its objective.

Index changes and rebalancing

Indexes are reviewed. Companies can be added, removed or given new weights. The fund then trades to follow the updated rules. This is not the same as a manager making a personal forecast, but it is also not “set once and never changed.”

Some index changes can cause significant trading. Read how often the index is reviewed and how the fund handles changes.

Home-country bias and currency risk

Investors often hold more of their home market because it feels familiar and matches local spending. Familiarity can be useful, but too much home-country exposure can make employment, property and investments depend on the same economy.

A global index can reduce one-country concentration but introduces foreign-market and currency movements. The fund’s trading currency does not by itself tell you whether currency exposure is hedged.

It is inaccurate to say index funds always win or active funds always win. The choice depends on the market, fund, cost, tax, behaviour and investor objective. Past results do not settle the future.

Questions to ask before choosing an index fund

  1. What exact index does it follow?
  2. What part of the market does that index leave out?
  3. How are holdings selected and weighted?
  4. How concentrated are the largest companies, sectors or countries?
  5. Is the fund an ETF, mutual fund or another structure?
  6. Does it hold every constituent, sample the index or use derivatives?
  7. What are the full costs and recent tracking difference?
  8. Is there currency exposure or hedging?
  9. How does it overlap with the rest of the portfolio?
  10. Does the risk fit the goal and time horizon?

Common mistakes

  • Assuming every index is broad and diversified.
  • Choosing only the index with the highest recent return.
  • Ignoring concentration in the largest constituents.
  • Comparing funds that follow different indexes as if fees were the only difference.
  • Thinking “passive” means no decisions or no changes.
  • Ignoring tax, platform and trading costs outside the fund’s headline fee.
  • Owning several index funds that largely repeat the same holdings.

How an index fund can fit a wider plan

An index fund is one possible building block, not a complete financial plan. The plan still needs an emergency reserve, a goal, a time horizon, an affordable contribution, an asset mix and a rule for reviewing progress. Read Investing for Beginners for that wider sequence and What Is an ETF? for the exchange-traded structure.

Frequently asked questions

Is every index fund an ETF?

No. An index fund can use an ETF, mutual-fund or another permitted structure. The strategy and wrapper are separate questions.

Do index funds always have low risk?

No. Risk comes from the assets and index. A narrow sector or volatile market can remain high risk even when the fund is passive.

Why can a fund return differ from its index?

Fees, taxes, trading, cash holdings, sampling, index changes and timing can create a tracking difference.
