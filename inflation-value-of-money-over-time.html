INFLATION GUIDE

What will your money be worth in 10, 20 and 30 years?

Inflation does not make the number in your account smaller, but it can reduce what that number is able to buy.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Test your purchasing power→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

Inflation reduces the purchasing power of a fixed amount of money. If prices rise 5% per year, $10,000 in the future buys less than $10,000 buys today. The correct comparison is between nominal money—the number printed on the account—and real money—what that amount can actually purchase.

BEFORE YOU START

Future money and today's money are not the same thing.

If prices rise over time, the same nominal amount buys fewer goods and services. This is why a goal of "1 million" is incomplete unless you know whether that number is stated in today's purchasing power or in future currency.

If inflation averaged 5% a yearAfter 10 years, prices would be about 1.63× today's level.After 20 years, prices would be about 2.65× today's level.After 30 years, prices would be about 4.32× today's level.

Equivalently, 100 of purchasing power today would correspond to only about 61 in today's purchasing-power terms after 10 years, 38 after 20 years and 23 after 30 years if prices rose at a constant 5% rate.

This is a scenario, not an inflation forecast

Real inflation changes from year to year, and different households experience different inflation. Healthcare, education, rent, food and travel can move at different rates. A planning calculator uses a constant assumption to make the scenario understandable, not because inflation is expected to be constant.

Why long-term goals are especially sensitive

For a goal 20 or 30 years away, even a one-percentage-point change in inflation can materially change the future amount. That is why retirement and education goals should be tested across more than one price-growth assumption.

Nominal return is not the same as real return

If an investment grows 8% while prices rise 5%, your purchasing power has not increased by the full 8%. The exact real return is approximately (1.08 / 1.05) - 1, or about 2.86%. This distinction matters when comparing future portfolio values with future expenses.

Keep units consistent: compare future money with future money, or today's money with today's money. Mixing the two can make a plan look better or worse than it really is.

How to use inflation in a plan

For a future goal, start with the cost today if that is easier to understand. Apply an inflation or price-growth assumption to estimate the future nominal target. Then solve for the savings path required to reach that future amount.

For retirement, consider whether different expense categories deserve different assumptions rather than applying one rate blindly to everything.

Future cost is the mirror image

If today’s basket costs $10,000, the future nominal amount required to buy that same basket is $10,000 × (1 + inflation)^years. Purchasing-power calculations discount future money back to today; future-cost calculations compound today’s price forward.

Future cost of today’s basketCurrent cost × (1 + inflation)^years

Inflation compounds even when the annual rate looks small

At 4% annual inflation, prices roughly double over about 18 years using the Rule of 72 as a rough mental shortcut. At 6%, the doubling time is around 12 years. This is why long-term goals can be underfunded even when the original target looked large.

A price index is an average, not your personal basket

Official inflation measures aggregate thousands of prices with particular weights. Your own spending pattern can differ. A renter, homeowner, student, retiree and frequent traveler can experience different changes in effective purchasing power even in the same country.

Nominal account growth can hide a real loss

If cash earns 3% while prices rise 5%, the account balance grows but purchasing power falls. The exact real return is about −1.9%, not simply −2%, because the two rates compound against each other.

Exact real return(1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Where the distinction matters most

  • Retirement targets spanning decades.
  • Education or housing goals with category-specific cost inflation.
  • Fixed pensions, annuities or bonds whose payments do not rise with prices.
  • Emergency funds held for long periods beyond their intended liquidity purpose.
  • Comparing investment returns across periods with different inflation environments.

Think in purchasing-power half-life

A useful mental model is how long it takes purchasing power to fall roughly by half. At 6% inflation, the price level roughly doubles in about 12 years, so a fixed nominal amount buys about half as much. At 3%, the process takes roughly 24 years. This is approximate, but it makes long-horizon erosion intuitive.

Base-year discipline prevents misleading comparisons

When charts show “today’s money,” all values should be converted to the same price-year. Comparing a 2045 nominal salary with a 2026 expense without conversion mixes two price levels. Label the base year so readers know what purchasing power the numbers represent.

Inflation can be volatile even when long-run averages look stable

A 30-year average of 4% can contain years near zero and years far above 4%. For a retiree or near-term goal, the path matters because a cluster of high inflation early can permanently raise the spending base. Averages are useful for planning but do not remove path risk.

Frequently asked questions

Does inflation mean money literally disappears?

No. The nominal amount is unchanged; its purchasing power falls because the same goods and services cost more.

Is the CPI exactly my personal inflation rate?

No. A consumer-price index is an average basket. Your effective rate depends on what you buy and where you live.

How do I compare a future amount with today’s money?

Discount the future nominal amount by cumulative inflation: future amount ÷ (1 + inflation)^years.

What if my savings interest rate equals inflation?

Before tax/fees, purchasing power is roughly preserved, though the exact real return is calculated as (1+return)/(1+inflation)-1.
