HOME-BUYING CASH PLAN

How much cash do you need to buy a home beyond the deposit?

A deposit is only one use of cash at purchase. A stronger home plan keeps separate buckets for transaction costs, moving and initial repairs, and an emergency reserve after completion.

By CarrowmontPublished 25 November 2026 · Reviewed 25 November 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy

30-second answer

Do not treat your entire savings balance as the down payment or deposit. Home purchases usually require additional cash for lender or legal fees, taxes or registration, valuation or inspection, moving and setup, and sometimes immediate repairs. You also need enough liquidity after purchase for normal emergencies. The categories are broadly similar across markets, but the actual amounts and taxes are country- and property-specific.

Stamp duty, registration, closing costs, mortgage insurance, legal fees, deposit requirements and lender charges vary substantially by country and loan type. Use local official or regulated sources for exact amounts; Carrowmont deliberately does not apply one country’s percentages globally.

Think in cash buckets, not one deposit number

A home purchase can fail the household budget even when the monthly mortgage looks affordable. The reason is that buying also consumes cash before and around completion. A safer plan separates the money into distinct buckets so the deposit does not accidentally consume funds needed for fees or emergencies.

The deposit affects the loan, but fees affect the cash plan

A larger deposit can reduce the loan amount and may improve pricing or insurance requirements in some markets. But using every available unit of cash for the deposit can leave the household unable to pay transaction costs or keep an emergency reserve. The best deposit is therefore not automatically the maximum amount you can transfer on completion day.

Closing, registration and legal costs vary by country

The United States commonly uses the term closing costs. The United Kingdom may include conveyancing, Stamp Duty or Land Tax, valuation and mortgage fees. Australia can involve stamp duty, conveyancing, inspections and lender costs. India may involve stamp duty, registration, processing, legal and technical charges. Other countries have their own structures.

Because these systems differ, Carrowmont does not hard-code one global percentage. Build a local checklist and obtain written estimates before treating your available cash as a deposit.

Do not forget the first months after purchase

Moving can expose costs that did not appear in the mortgage calculation: Repairs, appliances, utility deposits, maintenance, association or strata charges, insurance and higher commuting or energy costs. Some are predictable; others are not. Keep a post-purchase cash reserve rather than assuming the budget becomes stable on day one.

A worked framework

Suppose you have 120,000 of cash available. Instead of calling the entire 120,000 a deposit, divide it into planning buckets: Perhaps 80,000 for deposit, 15,000 for transaction and move costs, 5,000 for known immediate work and 20,000 kept as emergency cash. Those numbers are illustrative, not a recommendation. The important part is that the purchase does not silently spend the reserve twice.

Questions to ask before choosing the deposit

  1. What transaction taxes and registration charges apply to this property?
  2. What lender, valuation, legal and inspection fees are payable?
  3. Which costs must be paid in cash and which can be financed?
  4. How would a larger or smaller deposit change the rate, insurance or approval terms?
  5. What repairs or setup costs are known already?
  6. How much emergency cash will remain the day after completion?
  7. Can the monthly budget rebuild savings after the purchase?

What if the lender will finance some fees?

Financing a fee can reduce the cash needed today, but it can increase the loan balance or interest cost. A lender credit may also be linked to a higher interest rate. Compare the total cost, not just the immediate cash saving.

What if a bigger deposit gets a better rate?

That can be economically valuable, but compare the benefit with the liquidity you give up. A slightly better mortgage rate may not justify leaving the household with no emergency reserve. Test both the monthly payment and the post-purchase cash position.

Common mistakes

  • Using every saved unit as the deposit: Fees and repairs still need cash.
  • Copying another country’s cost percentage: Taxes and legal structures differ materially.
  • Ignoring costs that are added to the loan: Financing a fee does not make it free.
  • Forgetting the emergency fund: Homeownership can increase exposure to large repairs.
  • Planning only to completion day: The first months of ownership can be cash-intensive.

Frequently asked questions

Are closing costs always a fixed percentage?

No. Some sources publish rough ranges for specific markets, but actual costs depend on the country, property, loan and services required.

Should the emergency fund be part of the deposit?

Normally it is more useful to treat emergency savings as a separate reserve. Otherwise the purchase can leave the household with no accessible buffer.

Can I include repairs in the mortgage?

Some loan products may allow certain costs to be financed, but rules differ. Check the loan terms and compare the additional interest cost.

Does Carrowmont calculate stamp duty or registration tax?

No. Those amounts are country- and often region-specific and can change. Use current official local sources.
