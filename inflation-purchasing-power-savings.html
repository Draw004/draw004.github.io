PURCHASING POWER GUIDE

How much purchasing power can inflation take from your savings?

A balance can stay unchanged in nominal terms while its real buying power declines as prices rise.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Test the purchasing power of your money→

Direct answer

Savings grow in nominal terms at the interest or investment return credited to the account, but financial progress depends on real purchasing-power growth after inflation, tax and fees. A positive nominal return can still be a negative real return.

BEFORE YOU START

Nominal value and purchasing power are different.

If a savings balance does not grow while prices rise, the number in the account may be unchanged but the amount of goods and services it can buy can fall.

The reverse calculation is useful for long-term goals.

If you know the lifestyle or goal you want to preserve, inflation tells you approximately how large the future nominal amount may need to become.

Cash and investments solve different problems.

Accessible cash can provide stability and liquidity even if inflation erodes purchasing power. Long-term investments may seek growth but can fluctuate. A plan can need both.

Real return combines growth and inflation.

A nominal return is not the same as an increase in purchasing power. The real result depends on both investment growth and inflation.

Nominal return tells you the account change; real return tells you the purchasing-power change

Tax can make the real result worse

In many jurisdictions, interest or investment income can be taxable while inflation is not deductible from the gain. A 6% deposit return does not necessarily deliver 6% into the account after tax. Because rules vary by country and account type, use an after-tax return assumption when tax materially affects the goal.

Liquidity and purchasing power are different jobs

Emergency savings often prioritize stability and immediate access over maximum real return. It can be rational to accept some inflation erosion on a limited reserve because the money’s job is liquidity. Long-horizon goals have a different problem: holding all assets in low-yield cash can make inflation a larger risk.

The right comparison is against the goal’s inflation

If you are saving for a goal whose cost rises faster than general inflation, compare portfolio growth with that goal-specific rate. You can gain purchasing power against the general basket and still fall behind the cost of the goal.

Use different assets for different horizons

Investor.gov notes that very long horizons may have capacity for more investment risk because low-return savings products can lose purchasing power, while short-term goals cannot comfortably absorb a market loss just before the money is needed. This is a time-horizon trade-off, not a rule that everyone should hold the same asset mix.

A practical savings review

  • Identify the purpose and time horizon of each cash balance.
  • Estimate after-tax/fee nominal return.
  • Compare it with inflation relevant to the goal.
  • Keep emergency liquidity separate from long-term investment capital.
  • If real growth is negative for a long-term goal, adjust contribution, horizon or asset mix rather than merely focusing on the nominal interest rate.

Interest rates often respond to inflation with a lag

Cash yields can rise when central banks tighten policy, but the adjustment may occur after prices have already increased. A high savings-account rate therefore does not automatically mean purchasing power was protected over the whole inflation episode. Compare cumulative after-tax interest with cumulative inflation over the same period.

Real return should be goal-relative

General CPI is the right benchmark for broad purchasing power, but a house deposit can face house-price inflation, and education can face tuition inflation. The asset may beat CPI while still falling behind the actual liability. Measure progress against the thing the money is meant to buy.

Inflation risk and market risk trade off

Moving all long-term savings into volatile assets to “beat inflation” can create a loss right before the goal date. Holding everything in cash can create persistent real erosion. Time horizon determines which risk is more damaging, so asset choice should follow the liability date.

Measure the reserve and the investment portfolio by different scorecards

It is reasonable for an emergency reserve to “lose” some purchasing power if the alternative is exposing essential liquidity to large market losses. Judge that reserve by stability, accessibility and sufficiency. Judge a 20-year investment goal by expected real growth, diversification, fees and risk. Applying the same return target to both pools confuses their jobs and can lead either to an under-protected emergency fund or an overly conservative long-term portfolio.

Replenish real purchasing power after a long inflation episode

If essential monthly expenses rise from 60,000 to 75,000, a six-month reserve that remains at 360,000 is no longer six months. Resize liquidity from current essential spending rather than assuming the original nominal target remains valid forever.

Frequently asked questions

Is a savings account bad if it earns less than inflation?

Not necessarily. For emergency liquidity, stability and access can be more important than real growth. For a decades-long goal, persistent negative real returns are a bigger concern.

Can a high nominal interest rate still lose purchasing power?

Yes. If inflation and taxes/fees exceed the net return, purchasing power falls even though the account balance rises.

Should I subtract inflation from return?

Return minus inflation is a useful approximation for small rates. The exact formula is (1+return)/(1+inflation)-1.

Which inflation rate should I compare with?

Use broad inflation for general purchasing power, and consider goal-specific inflation when one category dominates the future expense.
