RETIREMENT · LEARNING GUIDE

Retirement spending is not simply today's budget plus inflation.

A useful retirement estimate starts by asking which expenses will still exist, which may end, and which may become more important later in life.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO USE YOUR OWN NUMBERS?Model your retirement expenses→

Use your own retirement age, expense assumptions and planning horizon.

Direct answer

Retirement expenses rarely equal current expenses minus commuting. A useful retirement budget separates costs that end, continue, begin or change shape, then maps large irregular expenses and potential late-life care costs onto the timeline. The objective is not to predict every bill perfectly, but to make the major spending drivers visible.

Some expenses may disappear

School fees, a mortgage, work commuting or other temporary obligations may end before retirement. If those costs are blindly inflated forever, the model can overstate the lifestyle you actually intend to fund.

Some expenses may continue at a different level

Utilities, groceries, housing, travel and domestic support can remain but not necessarily at today's level. A category may become smaller, stay similar, or increase depending on the lifestyle you expect.

Some expenses can rise faster than general inflation

Healthcare is a common example of a category people may wish to model separately. The important point is not to assume that any one category will definitely rise at a particular rate, but to make the assumption visible and adjustable.

Retirement income changes the amount your portfolio must provide

Pensions, rent, annuity income or other recurring income can reduce the amount that must be withdrawn from savings. It is useful to model those income sources separately rather than subtracting them vaguely from today's household budget.

A clearer retirement budget asks:Which costs end before retirement?Which costs continue, and at what level?Which categories use a different inflation assumption?What income begins in retirement?Are there one-time goals in addition to monthly living costs?

The first retirement year is not the whole retirement

A retirement plan may last decades. Expenses and income can change during that period. One-time goals may occur at specific ages, while lifestyle phases can change recurring spending. A year-by-year cash-flow view therefore provides more information than a single withdrawal-rate shortcut.

Important limitation: any corpus estimate depends on assumptions about inflation, returns, longevity, spending, income and timing. Actual investment returns and real-life expenses vary, so the result is an educational estimate rather than a guarantee.

Do not count saving as retirement spending

During working years, a high savings rate reduces take-home consumption. Once retired, contributions to the retirement portfolio usually stop, so replacing 100% of salary can overstate lifestyle needs. On the other hand, taxes and healthcare can offset some of that reduction. A bottom-up budget exposes both effects.

The spending path may be non-linear

Some research and practitioner frameworks describe a “go-go, slow-go, no-go” or horseshoe-like pattern: discretionary travel/activity can be higher early, fall later, while healthcare/care costs can rise at advanced ages. It is a scenario, not a universal law. Model at least one alternative to constant real spending.

Essential versus flexible spending is a retirement risk tool

If basic housing, food, utilities, insurance and care needs consume nearly the entire budget, there is little room to respond to a market shock. If travel, gifting and upgrades are explicitly flexible, the plan can define temporary reductions rather than making emergency decisions under stress.

Housing deserves a lifecycle view

  • Mortgage repayment may end, but maintenance, insurance, taxes/fees and utilities continue.
  • Rent may keep rising with local housing inflation.
  • Downsizing has transaction costs and may not release as much cash as expected.
  • A later move closer to family or care services can change both housing and healthcare costs.

Healthcare and care need a separate stress scenario

Country systems differ greatly, so avoid one global healthcare number. Instead identify insurance premiums, deductibles/out-of-pocket expenses, medicines, dental/vision/hearing, home support and potential long-term care. Then test a higher late-life annual amount even if the base case assumes lower spending.

Create a retirement expense timeline

  1. Estimate the first retirement-year budget in today’s money.
  2. Split essential and flexible categories.
  3. Add one-off goals and replacement cycles by year.
  4. Add known debt payoff or pension-start dates.
  5. Stress late-life health/care spending and longevity.
  6. Inflate the cash flows consistently and compare them with income sources and portfolio capacity.

Build phase changes explicitly rather than applying one percentage forever

A retirement budget can be modeled in phases: early active years, middle years, and later years with different travel, transport and care assumptions. This avoids the opposite errors of assuming spending never changes or assuming everyone follows the same age-based decline.

Add shock reserves outside the recurring budget

A recurring budget should not absorb every possible roof repair, family emergency or medical event. A separate contingency reserve makes the base lifestyle estimate cleaner and reduces the chance that one large expense is mistaken for a permanently higher annual run rate.

Caregiving can affect both spending and time

Supporting a spouse, parent or adult child can create direct costs and reduce the household’s ability to earn part-time income. This is difficult to predict, so include it as a scenario rather than pretending the base budget captures every late-life obligation.

Frequently asked questions

Should I assume retirement expenses are 70% or 80% of salary?

Use that only as a rough comparison. A bottom-up budget better captures mortgage status, taxes, healthcare, travel, savings that stop, and household-specific commitments.

Do expenses always fall as retirees age?

No. Some discretionary activity may fall, but healthcare, care and home-support costs can rise. Model more than one spending path.

How should I include large home or vehicle expenses?

Treat predictable replacement and repair costs as sinking funds or explicit future cash flows rather than hoping the monthly average absorbs them.

Why split essential and discretionary expenses?

It shows how much spending can be adjusted in response to weak markets or high inflation, which directly affects retirement-plan resilience.
