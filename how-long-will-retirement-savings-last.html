RETIREMENT DURATION GUIDE

How long could your retirement savings last?

The answer depends on withdrawals, inflation, investment assumptions, retirement income and how long the plan needs to run.

Direct answer

How long retirement savings last depends on the interaction of withdrawals, returns, inflation and the order in which returns occur. A simple “balance ÷ annual spending” calculation is useful only when return and inflation are both ignored. A robust plan tests multiple return paths and spending responses.

BEFORE YOU START

A portfolio can last for very different periods under different withdrawal paths.

A starting balance by itself does not tell you how long retirement savings may last. The result depends on how much is withdrawn, whether withdrawals rise with inflation, investment returns and other retirement income.

Inflation can make a fixed lifestyle require rising withdrawals.

Keeping the same nominal withdrawal every year may mean accepting lower purchasing power. Increasing withdrawals with inflation can preserve the modelled lifestyle but puts more pressure on the portfolio over time.

Sequence matters even when the average return looks reasonable.

A smooth calculator assumption is useful for planning, but real returns are uneven. Poor returns early in retirement can be harder to recover from when withdrawals are happening at the same time.

Plan to an age, not only a number of years.

Instead of asking whether the money lasts 20 years, consider the age at which retirement begins and the age through which you want the plan to remain funded. Testing a longer horizon can reveal how sensitive the plan is.

The no-return calculation is only a starting point

If a portfolio contains ₹1 crore and withdrawals stay fixed at ₹5 lakh per year, dividing one by the other gives 20 years. But investment returns can extend the runway, while inflation raises withdrawals and poor early returns can shorten it.

Average return is not enough once withdrawals begin

During accumulation, the order of returns matters less when there are no withdrawals. During retirement, a loss can force you to sell more units to fund the same spending. Those units are no longer present when markets recover. This is sequence-of-returns risk.

Model a spending policy, not just a withdrawal amount

A rigid inflation-adjusted withdrawal is easy to model, but retirees often change behavior. Percentage-of-portfolio, guardrail and dynamic-spending approaches deliberately vary spending with portfolio conditions. Flexibility can improve durability, but it makes annual income less predictable.

Account for income that starts later

A portfolio may need to fund all spending for the first five years, then less after a pension or public benefit begins. Treating the entire retirement as one constant withdrawal rate can therefore overstate or understate the burden in different periods.

Use three tests for “Will it last?”

  1. Deterministic baseline: a transparent return/inflation path so you can understand the mechanics.
  2. Adverse sequence: weak returns in the first five to ten years while withdrawals continue.
  3. Longevity + inflation stress: several extra years of life combined with higher-than-base spending inflation.

What to monitor after retirement begins

  • Current withdrawal amount ÷ current portfolio value.
  • Essential-spending coverage from stable income sources.
  • Portfolio allocation and liquidity reserve.
  • Whether actual inflation is materially above the planning assumption.
  • Large changes in health, housing, family support or taxes.
  • Whether a planned discretionary expense can be delayed after a weak market.

Track the current withdrawal rate as the plan evolves

The initial withdrawal rate is set on retirement day, but the current withdrawal rate changes as spending and portfolio value change. If markets fall while withdrawals rise with inflation, the current rate can jump sharply. That is a useful warning signal even though no single threshold is universally “unsafe.”

Terminal value is part of the design

A plan that intentionally spends most assets by a chosen age is different from a plan that aims to preserve real principal for heirs or late-life care. The same starting portfolio can support more spending if a low terminal value is acceptable, but the risk of living longer must then be handled elsewhere.

Do not ignore cash-flow timing within the year

Annual models assume withdrawals occur at a convenient point. Monthly withdrawals during a fast market decline can produce slightly different outcomes. For household planning, the bigger issue is whether enough near-term liquidity exists to avoid forced selling during stress.

“Running out” is not the only failure mode

A portfolio can remain above zero and still fail the household if spending must be cut below essential needs, if a surviving spouse loses income, or if there is insufficient liquidity for care. Define the minimum acceptable lifestyle and late-life reserve rather than using a positive terminal balance as the only success test.

Review after unusually strong markets too

Risk management is not only for downturns. A large market gain can reduce the current withdrawal rate and create room for spending, gifting or de-risking—but permanently increasing fixed spending after one strong year can recreate fragility. Use the same decision framework in good markets that you would want in bad ones.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just divide my portfolio by annual expenses?

Only as a zero-return, zero-inflation baseline. It does not capture portfolio growth, rising costs or sequence risk.

Does a 6% average return mean my plan can safely spend 6%?

No. Average return and sustainable withdrawal rate are different concepts because returns are volatile, inflation matters and withdrawals occur along the path.

What if I have a pension starting later?

Model the portfolio as a bridge until the pension begins, then reduce the portfolio-funded spending by the after-tax income you expect from that source.

Should I recalculate after retirement?

Yes. Remaining horizon, portfolio value, spending and market conditions change. A withdrawal plan is a process, not a number chosen once on retirement day.
