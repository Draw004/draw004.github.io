EMERGENCY FUND GUIDE

How much emergency fund should you keep?

An emergency fund is about liquidity and resilience, not maximizing long-term investment returns.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Plan an emergency savings target→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

There is no universal emergency-fund number. A more useful estimate starts with essential monthly expenses and then adjusts the number of months for income stability, dependants, insurance, debt commitments and how quickly you could replace lost income.

BEFORE YOU START

Start with essential monthly expenses, not a round headline number.

An emergency fund is designed to cover costs that cannot easily be postponed if income stops or an unexpected expense appears.

The right buffer depends on how predictable your income and expenses are.

A household with two stable incomes may choose a different buffer from someone with variable income, a single income source or large unavoidable commitments.

Liquidity has a purpose.

Emergency money is usually intended to be accessible when needed. That means its role is different from long-horizon investing, where short-term price movement may be acceptable.

Keep known future expenses separate.

A planned insurance premium, school fee or annual trip is not really an emergency. Creating separate sinking funds can stop predictable costs from consuming the emergency reserve.

Base the calculation on essential expenses, not total lifestyle spending

The purpose of an emergency fund is to keep the household functioning through a shock. Start with housing, basic food, utilities, essential transport, insurance, minimum debt payments and other unavoidable commitments. Discretionary travel, entertainment and optional upgrades can usually be reduced temporarily.

Three months is a reference point, not a commandment

Emergency-fund guidance often uses “months of expenses” because it links the reserve to the household’s actual cost base. Surveys also commonly track whether households can cover several months of expenses. But the right number can be lower or higher depending on how predictable income is and how costly a disruption would be.

Illustrative householdEssential monthly expenses: $6,000.Three-month buffer: $18,000.Six-month buffer: $36,000.The decision between those amounts depends on risk capacity and household circumstances, not on a universal percentage.

Liquidity matters as much as the headline amount

Emergency money should be accessible when the emergency occurs. A high expected return is less useful if accessing the money requires selling a volatile asset after a large decline, paying a penalty, or waiting through a lock-up period. The reserve’s job is resilience, not maximum return.

Build it in stages if the full target feels large

  1. Create a first-line buffer for common unexpected bills.
  2. Grow it toward one month of essential expenses.
  3. Extend the reserve based on income and household risk.
  4. Recalculate after major changes such as a new mortgage, dependant, job change or move.

The right reserve is driven by risk exposure, not a universal number of months

“Three to six months” is a useful conversation starter, but the required buffer can differ greatly. A household with two stable incomes, low fixed costs and strong insurance has a different liquidity risk from a single-income household with variable commissions, dependants and high debt payments.

Tier the emergency reserve

A practical design can keep a small instant-access layer for routine shocks and a larger secondary layer in highly liquid, low-volatility instruments appropriate to the country. This separates “needed tonight” money from “needed after several weeks of income loss” without treating long-term investments as cash.

Define the replenishment rule before using it

If an emergency draws the reserve down, decide how normal contributions will rebuild it and which discretionary goals temporarily slow. Otherwise the fund can remain permanently depleted after one event.

Insurance deductibles and waiting periods belong in the reserve calculation

A household can be well insured and still need significant cash. Policies may have deductibles, exclusions, reimbursement delays or waiting periods. The emergency reserve should be able to bridge those gaps without relying on a credit line that may become expensive or unavailable during the same shock.

Plan for correlated shocks

The most damaging emergencies can arrive together: a recession can reduce income while markets fall and credit conditions tighten. This is why counting a volatile investment portfolio or unused credit-card limit as equivalent to cash can overstate resilience. Stress the reserve against a scenario where several backup resources are weaker at the same time.

Frequently asked questions

Should I keep exactly six months of expenses?

Not necessarily. Six months is a common rule of thumb, but the useful number depends on your income stability, essential costs, household responsibilities and other financial buffers.

Can investments count as an emergency fund?

They may be part of your broader resources, but volatile or illiquid investments can fall in value or be hard to access precisely when cash is needed. A dedicated liquid reserve has a different job.

Should I calculate from gross salary?

A reserve is usually easier to size from essential expenses because those are the cash outflows the fund must cover during a disruption.
