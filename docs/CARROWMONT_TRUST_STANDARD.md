# Carrowmont Trust Standard

## Purpose

Carrowmont exists to help people understand financial decisions more clearly.

That means every calculator, article, research page, newsletter, social post and market commentary should make it easy for a reader to understand:

- what is known
- what is assumed
- what is only a possible scenario
- what is a forecast
- where the information came from
- when the information was last checked
- what uncertainty remains

Carrowmont should never make uncertainty look like certainty.

---

# 1. Facts

A **fact** is information that can be verified from a reliable source.

Examples:

“On October 28, 2026, the Federal Reserve maintained its target range at 3.75%–4.00%.”

“Inflation was X% according to the latest official release.”

Whenever a fact may change over time, Carrowmont should show:

**Source:** Federal Reserve  
**As of:** 28 October 2026

Where possible, prefer primary or highly credible sources such as central banks, government statistics agencies, regulators, exchanges, multilateral institutions and company filings.

---

# 2. Assumptions

An **assumption** is an input used to perform a calculation or illustrate an outcome.

Examples:

“Assumes a 6% annual investment return.”

“Assumes inflation averages 3% per year.”

“Assumes monthly contributions remain unchanged.”

Assumptions must never be presented as predictions.

Where an assumption materially changes the result, users should be able to change it wherever practical.

Carrowmont should clearly explain that actual outcomes may differ.

---

# 3. Scenarios

A **scenario** describes what could happen if certain conditions occur.

Examples:

“If mortgage rates rise by 1 percentage point…”

“If inflation remains at 5% for another three years…”

“If you increase your monthly contribution by 10%…”

Preferred wording:

**If…**  
**Under this scenario…**  
**This could result in…**  
**For illustration…**

Avoid:

**This will happen.**

---

# 4. Forecasts

A **forecast** is an informed expectation about the future.

Forecasts should always identify who made the forecast and when.

Example:

“Markets were pricing a higher probability of a rate increase as of 31 October 2026.”

Or:

“According to the IMF’s October 2026 forecast…”

Carrowmont should never convert someone else’s forecast into a Carrowmont certainty.

Preferred wording:

“Forecasts suggest…”  
“Markets currently expect…”  
“Analysts estimate…”  
“One possible outcome is…”

---

# 5. Investment Returns

Carrowmont must never imply that an investment return is guaranteed unless the product itself legally provides a guaranteed contractual return and that guarantee is clearly verified.

Avoid language such as:

“This stock will double.”

“You will earn 12%.”

“Gold will definitely rise.”

“This is a guaranteed multibagger.”

Preferred language:

“Historically, this asset has returned…”

“A 10% return is used here as an illustration.”

“If returns average 8%…”

“This investment could benefit if…”

“There is also a risk that…”

Expected returns in calculators must always be labelled as assumptions.

---

# 6. Calculator Results

Calculator outputs are estimates, not promises.

Where appropriate, results should include wording such as:

**Illustrative result based on the assumptions entered. Actual outcomes may differ.**

Important assumptions should be visible rather than hidden.

Whenever possible, Carrowmont should allow users to compare multiple scenarios instead of giving one supposedly “correct” answer.

---

# 7. Inflation and Cost of Living

Official inflation data should be treated as an important economic measure, but Carrowmont should also recognise that individual households can experience inflation differently.

A household’s actual cost-of-living change depends on what that household spends money on.

Carrowmont may therefore distinguish between:

**Reported inflation** — official statistical measure

and

**Lived inflation** — how price changes may feel to an individual household based on its spending pattern

The two should not be presented as contradictory simply because they differ.

---

# 8. Market Commentary

Carrowmont should explain markets rather than pretend to predict them perfectly.

Market commentary should separate:

**What happened**

from

**Why it may have happened**

from

**What could happen next**

When the reason for a market movement is uncertain, say so.

Avoid attributing every rise or fall to a single cause unless evidence clearly supports it.

---

# 9. Sources

Material financial claims should come from credible sources.

Priority should generally be given to:

Primary sources → official institutions → regulatory filings → established financial publications → reputable research organisations.

Social-media posts may help identify a development, but important claims should be checked against stronger underlying evidence where possible.

For time-sensitive information, always record the date.

---

# 10. Freshness

Financial information becomes outdated quickly.

Every time-sensitive page or article should make clear when the information was last reviewed.

Where appropriate:

**Last updated:** 1 November 2026

Carrowmont should not describe information as “current,” “today,” or “latest” without verifying it.

---

# 11. Headlines

Headlines should attract attention without exaggerating the underlying evidence.

Avoid:

“Market crash is coming.”

“This investment will make you rich.”

“Retire with ₹1 crore guaranteed.”

“Fed rate hike confirmed” when no decision has been made.

Prefer:

“What a December Fed rate increase could mean for households”

“Could higher oil prices keep inflation elevated?”

“What happens to retirement savings under different return assumptions?”

---

# 12. Human Impact

Carrowmont should connect financial developments to real life.

Interest rates are not only percentages.

Inflation is not only a statistic.

Market declines are not only charts.

Behind financial numbers are decisions about homes, families, education, healthcare, retirement, work and financial security.

Carrowmont should explain that human impact without creating fear.

---

# 13. Social Media

Carrowmont social posts should remain conversational, understandable and grounded.

The goal is not to sound like an economist speaking to another economist.

The goal is to help an ordinary person understand:

**What happened?**

**Why might it matter to me?**

**What should I think about next?**

Opinions should be clearly distinguishable from facts.

Questions and uncertainty are preferable to false certainty.

---

# 14. Carrowmont Money in Real Life

Every weekly newsletter should follow three principles:

### Key developments
What materially changed this week?

### Real-life impact
What could it mean for households and everyday financial decisions?

### Practical next steps
What can the reader consider, review or calculate?

If there is not enough meaningful news in a particular week, Carrowmont should publish fewer items rather than manufacture importance.

Quality is more important than filling space.

---

# 15. Financial Decisions

Carrowmont should help people understand choices rather than tell every reader that one decision is universally correct.

Whenever possible:

Show alternatives.

Explain trade-offs.

Show assumptions.

Discuss downside risks.

Let the user compare scenarios.

A decision that is suitable for one household may not be suitable for another.

---

# 16. Educational Position

Carrowmont provides financial education, calculators, research and scenario analysis.

Content should not be presented as personalised financial, investment, tax or legal advice unless Carrowmont later develops the required professional and regulatory framework to offer those services.

Where appropriate:

**For educational and informational purposes. Individual circumstances may differ.**

---

# 17. Carrowmont Language Standard

Use:

**may**

**could**

**scenario**

**assumption**

**illustration**

**historically**

**based on current information**

**as of [date]**

**under these assumptions**

Use certainty only when the underlying fact is actually certain.

---

# 18. The Carrowmont Test

Before publishing anything, ask:

**Is it true?**

**Can we support it?**

**Is the source credible?**

**Is the date clear?**

**Have we separated fact from assumption?**

**Have we separated forecast from certainty?**

**Have we explained the downside as well as the upside?**

**Would an ordinary reader understand it?**

**Could someone reasonably mistake this for a promise?**

**Does it help someone make a clearer decision?**

If the answer to the final question is no, the content probably does not belong on Carrowmont.

---

## Core Principle

**Carrowmont should never try to appear more certain than the evidence allows.**

Our credibility should come from explaining uncertainty clearly — not hiding it.

**Real questions. Clearer answers.**  
**Financial Planning for Real Life.**
