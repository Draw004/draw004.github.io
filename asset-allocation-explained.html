What is asset allocation, and why does it matter?

Asset allocation is the decision about how much of a portfolio sits in different asset classes, not a prediction about which asset will win next.

CarrowmontPublished 27 September 2026 · Reviewed 21 October 2026Editorial standardsEducational planning guide

Direct answer

Asset allocation is the decision about how much of a portfolio is exposed to different asset classes such as shares, bonds and cash. A sensible starting point is the goal, time horizon, liquidity need and ability to absorb loss—not whichever asset performed best recently.

START WITH THE GOAL

Asset allocation is a risk-budget decision.

Different assets respond differently to economic conditions and carry different kinds of risk. Asset allocation is the portfolio-level decision about how those exposures are combined. It does not tell you which individual security to buy, and it cannot remove investment risk.

Asset classPossible role in a planImportant risks to understand
Shares / equitiesLong-term participation in business growthLarge price falls, business risk, valuation risk and concentration
Bonds / fixed incomeContractual income and potential portfolio stabilityInterest-rate risk, credit/default risk, inflation and liquidity
Cash / short-term depositsNear-term spending, emergency liquidity and lower price volatilityInflation may reduce purchasing power; provider and access terms matter
Property, commodities and other assetsMay add different economic exposuresCan be illiquid, concentrated, cyclical, leveraged or expensive

The table describes possible roles, not a recommended portfolio. Products, tax treatment, protections and costs differ by country.

Risk tolerance and risk capacity are not the same.

Risk tolerance is the emotional willingness to live with uncertainty and losses. Risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb those losses without derailing the goal. A person can feel comfortable with volatility but still have low capacity for loss if a home deposit is needed next year. Someone else may dislike volatility but have high capacity because retirement is decades away and the goal is flexible.

QuestionMostly about toleranceMostly about capacity
How would a 20% fall make you feel?YesPartly
When will the money be needed?PartlyYes
Could a loss force you to delay or cancel the goal?NoYes
Would you abandon the plan during a market fall?YesPartly

The same person can reasonably use different allocations for different goals.

Two-goal illustrationGoal A: a home deposit needed in three years. The date is fairly fixed and a large loss could directly reduce the amount available for the purchase.Goal B: retirement in 25 years. The horizon is much longer, contributions can continue and the spending date may have some flexibility.The two goals have different loss capacity even though the investor is the same person. That is why “one portfolio based only on age” can miss important context.

Time horizon matters, but it is not enough by itself.

A longer horizon may give an investor more time to recover from market declines, but horizon is only one input. Liquidity needs, job security, debt, dependants, insurance, goal flexibility and the size of the loss relative to total resources can all affect risk capacity.

Rebalancing is maintenance, not a market forecast.

Market movements can change a portfolio's mix. If a hypothetical portfolio started at 60% growth assets and 40% defensive assets and later drifted to 70%/30%, rebalancing would mean moving it back toward its intended risk mix. The 60/40 example is only a teaching illustration, not a recommended allocation.

Rebalancing can be done on a calendar schedule, when allocations move beyond chosen bands, or by directing new contributions toward underweight areas. Taxes, dealing costs and account rules can influence the method.

Labels such as “conservative” are not universal.

One provider's “balanced” portfolio can hold a different mix from another provider's portfolio with the same label. Read the actual underlying exposures, currency, credit quality, duration and concentration rather than relying on the product name alone.

Checklist: Build the allocation around the job the money must do

  • What is the specific goal for this money?
  • When might withdrawals begin, and how fixed is that date?
  • How much loss can the goal absorb without being delayed or reduced?
  • How much volatility can you realistically tolerate without abandoning the plan?
  • Do you need a separate emergency or near-term cash reserve?
  • Are you concentrated in one employer, property, country, sector or currency?
  • What rule will you use to review or rebalance the allocation?
  • Could fees, tax or account restrictions materially change implementation in your country?

Common allocation mistakes.

  • Choosing a mix because an asset class recently performed well.
  • Using the same risk level for a three-year goal and a 30-year goal without explanation.
  • Confusing willingness to take risk with financial ability to take risk.
  • Assuming a fund label tells you the actual underlying allocation.
  • Changing the allocation repeatedly in response to headlines rather than a written review rule.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one correct asset allocation for my age?

No. Age can influence horizon, but goals, liquidity, loss capacity, income stability and other resources also matter.

How often should an allocation be rebalanced?

There is no universal schedule. A calendar rule or tolerance band can create discipline, but implementation costs and tax rules should be considered.

Does asset allocation prevent losses?

No. It changes the mix of risks. Diversification can reduce some concentration risks, but broad market losses can still affect several asset classes at the same time.

Should every goal use the same allocation?

Not necessarily. Goals with different dates, flexibility and loss capacity can justify different risk budgets.
