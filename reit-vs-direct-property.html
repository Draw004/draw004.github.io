REIT vs direct property: what are the key differences?

Both can create real-estate exposure, but one is a pooled security and the other is direct ownership of a specific property.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide

Direct answer

REITs and direct property provide exposure to real estate through different ownership structures. REITs trade control and physical possession for liquidity, professional management and easier diversification; direct property offers control and potentially tailored leverage, but requires much more capital, operating work and tolerance for illiquidity.

The biggest difference is the ownership structure.

With direct property you own a specific asset and make or delegate operating decisions. With a REIT you own units or shares in a vehicle that owns or finances a portfolio of real-estate assets.

Liquidity and price visibility are different.

Listed REIT units can usually be traded on an exchange, though liquidity varies. Direct property transactions can take much longer and involve substantial legal, brokerage, tax and financing costs.

Control comes with responsibility.

Direct ownership can give more control over tenant selection, improvements, financing and timing of sale. That control also creates maintenance, vacancy, legal and administrative work. REIT investors delegate those decisions.

Leverage sits in different places

A direct-property investor may borrow against the specific property. A REIT also commonly uses debt, but the leverage is inside the listed vehicle. An investor comparing the two should examine both layers so the REIT is not incorrectly treated as “unleveraged” while the property is explicitly leveraged.

Liquidity changes behaviour and valuation

REIT prices can move sharply day to day because investors can trade instantly. Direct properties are repriced only when appraised or sold, which can make their risk feel lower. The economic value can still change between transactions. Liquidity is a feature, not proof of greater underlying risk.

Direct control can create both opportunity and concentration

A property owner can renovate, choose tenants, refinance or reposition an asset. Those decisions can add value if executed well, but the result depends heavily on one asset, one location and the owner’s operational skill. A diversified REIT portfolio gives up that control in exchange for professional management and scale.

The tax comparison is jurisdiction-specific

REIT distributions and property income can receive different tax treatment across countries. Direct ownership may have deductions, depreciation or transaction taxes that do not apply the same way to REITs. A global guide should therefore explain the structure and tell readers to verify current local rules rather than claiming one route is tax-efficient everywhere.

A household-balance-sheet view can change the answer

Someone who already owns a home and works in the property sector may already have large real-estate exposure. Adding a REIT is not automatically “diversification” simply because it is a security; it can increase the same underlying economic exposure. Look through the label to the real risk factor.

The control-versus-diversification trade-off is deeper than convenience

Direct ownership lets the investor choose the building, tenant strategy, financing and renovation. That control can create value if the owner has skill and time, but it also concentrates execution risk. REIT investors delegate those decisions to professional managers and gain liquidity, while accepting governance and market-price risk.

Financing behaves differently

A homeowner or landlord may have a mortgage tied to one property and personal credit. A REIT borrows at the corporate/vehicle level across a portfolio. Both use leverage, but the terms, refinancing schedule and consequences differ. Compare debt ratios and interest coverage rather than assuming “REIT = unleveraged.”

Tax should be checked after the structural decision

Some jurisdictions tax rental income, REIT distributions and capital gains differently. Because rules change, first decide which ownership form fits liquidity, control and diversification needs; then model the current local tax consequences with an authoritative source or professional.

Frequently asked questions

Which is more liquid?

A listed REIT is generally far more liquid than an individual property, although market conditions and specific products matter.

Which gives more control?

Direct property. REIT investors delegate asset selection and operations to management.

Can REITs be diversified if I own only one REIT?

It depends on the REIT. Some own many assets across markets; others are concentrated in one sector or geography. Look at the underlying portfolio.

Does direct property always produce higher returns because of leverage?

No. Leverage magnifies both gains and losses and adds financing cost. Higher return is not guaranteed.
