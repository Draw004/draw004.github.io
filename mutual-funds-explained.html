Mutual funds explained: How they work, cost money and should be compared

A mutual fund is a container. The important decision is what it owns, how it is managed, what it costs and whether that role fits your plan.

30-second answer

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it according to a stated objective. Buying a fund does not remove investment risk. It changes how you hold a portfolio: One fund can own many securities, but your result still depends on the underlying assets, concentration, fees, manager or index approach, tax treatment and how long you stay invested.

Fund structures, tax rules, account names, disclosure documents and investor-protection arrangements differ by country. This guide explains the common mechanics. Check the local prospectus or official disclosure before investing.

Start with the container, then look through it

A mutual fund is not an asset class by itself. A fund can own shares, bonds, cash-like instruments, property securities or a mix. Two funds with the same “mutual fund” label can therefore behave very differently. A broad equity index fund and a short-term bond fund are both funds, but their risk, return drivers and time horizons are not interchangeable.

Each investor owns units or shares representing a proportionate interest in the fund. The fund’s portfolio rises and falls in value as its holdings change in price and generate income. That means the useful question is not simply “Is a mutual fund safe?” It is “What does this fund own, and what risks do those holdings create?”

How NAV turns a portfolio into a per-unit value

Net asset value, or NAV, is the value of the fund’s assets after liabilities. A per-unit NAV divides that amount by the number of units or shares outstanding. Traditional open-end mutual funds generally transact with investors using a NAV calculated according to their market’s rules, often once each business day rather than continuously during the trading day.

Per-unit NAV(Fund assets − Fund liabilities) ÷ Units or shares outstanding

NAV is an accounting value, not a score of whether a fund is cheap or expensive. A fund with an NAV of 20 is not automatically “cheaper” than a fund with an NAV of 200. What matters is what each unit represents and how the underlying portfolio changes.

Where your return can come from

  • Income: Interest or dividends earned by the portfolio may be distributed or reinvested, depending on the fund and share class.
  • Realized gains: A fund may distribute gains when it sells holdings above their purchase cost, subject to local rules.
  • Change in NAV: If the value of the holdings rises after expenses and liabilities, the per-unit value can rise. It can also fall.

Do not confuse a distribution with “free return.” When value leaves a fund as a distribution, the fund’s value adjusts. The economic result is the combination of distributions and changes in the value of the units you still own, after costs and taxes.

Active and passive describe the method, not the risk level

ApproachWhat the fund is trying to doWhat to examine
Passive / indexTrack a defined index or rules-based benchmarkIndex design, tracking difference, fees, concentration and turnover
ActiveSelect or weight holdings differently from a benchmarkProcess, manager consistency, fees, portfolio changes and whether any added value survives costs
Multi-asset / target-dateCombine several asset types, sometimes changing the mix over timeUnderlying allocation, glide path, layered fees and whether the mix fits your goal

A passive fund can still be highly risky if it tracks a concentrated or volatile market. An active fund can be conservative if it invests in lower-risk assets. “Active versus passive” is a management choice; “high versus low risk” comes mainly from the underlying portfolio.

Mutual fund vs ETF: The portfolio can be similar, but trading is different

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds can hold very similar portfolios. The important structural difference is usually how investors buy and sell. A traditional mutual fund is typically purchased or redeemed with the fund at the applicable NAV. An ETF is traded on an exchange during market hours, so its market price can temporarily be above or below its NAV. Brokerage, bid-ask spreads, platform access and local tax rules can therefore matter differently.

Fees are small percentages applied repeatedly

Fund fees reduce the amount left to compound. Costs can include an ongoing expense ratio or management charge, transaction costs inside the portfolio, sales or platform charges, and other fees disclosed by the fund. The names vary by jurisdiction, so compare the total economic cost rather than one label.

Illustrative fee example

Suppose 100,000 grows at a smooth 6% a year before fund costs for 20 years. If annual fund costs were 0.25%, the simplified ending value would be about 305,920. At 1.25%, it would be about 252,977 — roughly 52,943 less. Real markets do not deliver smooth returns, and taxes and trading costs can differ, but the example shows why a recurring percentage cost matters over time.

The lesson is not “always buy the lowest-fee fund.” A cheaper fund can still be unsuitable. The lesson is that a higher-cost fund needs to justify a higher hurdle because the cost is deducted regardless of whether the fund performs well.

Diversification helps, but the fund name can hide concentration

A fund holding hundreds of securities may reduce the damage from one company failing, yet it can still be concentrated in one country, sector, style, currency or factor. A market-cap-weighted index can also become dominated by its largest companies. Look at the actual top holdings and sector or country weights. Owning several funds does not guarantee diversification if those funds own many of the same securities.

A practical way to choose a mutual fund

  1. Define the job first. Is this money for emergency liquidity, a three-year goal, retirement growth, income or portfolio diversification?
  2. Choose the asset mix before the fund. Decide whether the job calls for cash-like assets, bonds, equities or a combination.
  3. Identify the benchmark or mandate. Understand what the fund is supposed to own and what would make it behave differently from the market you think you are buying.
  4. Read the cost disclosure. Compare ongoing fees, purchase or redemption charges, platform costs and any layered fees.
  5. Inspect concentration and turnover. Top holdings, sector weights and frequent portfolio changes can alter both risk and tax outcomes.
  6. Compare like with like. Do not compare a small-company equity fund with a broad bond fund just because one has a higher historical return.
  7. Check the worst periods, not only the best. Past performance cannot predict future returns, but historical volatility and drawdowns can reveal how uncomfortable the ride has been.
  8. Confirm local tax and account rules. The same fund can produce a different after-tax result depending on the account and jurisdiction.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the fund with the highest recent return.
  • Assuming a low NAV means a fund is cheap.
  • Owning many overlapping funds and calling that diversification.
  • Ignoring fees because each annual percentage looks small.
  • Using a long-term equity fund for money needed soon.
  • Assuming “index fund” means low risk without checking the index.
  • Buying a themed fund without understanding how concentrated the theme is.

Frequently asked questions

Can a mutual fund lose money?

Yes. The value of the underlying holdings can fall, and fund investments are not the same as insured bank deposits. Risk depends on what the fund owns.

Is an index fund always better than an active fund?

No universal answer exists. Costs, benchmark design, tax effects, market exposure, manager process and the role in your portfolio all matter. The comparison should be made within the same asset category and objective.

Should I judge a fund by one-year performance?

No. A strong recent year can reflect a style, sector or market cycle that may not repeat. Start with objective, holdings, risk and costs, then use performance history as context rather than a forecast.

How many funds do I need?

There is no magic number. One broad multi-asset or broad-market fund can contain many holdings, while ten narrow funds can still overlap heavily. Measure exposure, not the number of fund names.
