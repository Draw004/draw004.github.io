SAVING RATE GUIDE

How much should you save each month?

The right saving amount is the one that funds important goals without making the current budget repeatedly fail.

CarrowmontUpdated 27 September 2026Educational planning guide

A general saving rate becomes more useful when each major goal has a target amount and date.

There is no universal percentage that fits every household.

The 50/30/20 rule uses 20% of take-home income for savings goals as one common reference point, but the CFPB describes it as a rule of thumb rather than a requirement for everyone. Housing costs, debt, income stability, dependants, age and goals can all justify a different path.

Build the amount from four questions

  • Do you have enough liquidity for unexpected expenses?
  • Are expensive debts or overdue obligations consuming the budget?
  • What goals have dates and target amounts?
  • How much recurring surplus remains after realistic current spending?

Separate emergency saving from long-term saving

Emergency reserves are designed for near-term shocks and should not depend on optimistic investment returns. Long-term retirement or FI contributions can usually tolerate a longer horizon. Giving them separate labels prevents one goal from silently funding another.

A target-based method is more precise than a generic percentage

Monthly goal allocation = future funding gap / remaining periods, adjusted for any return assumption used in the plan

For investment goals, a calculator may model growth rather than simple division. The return assumption is uncertain, so test more than one scenario instead of treating the result as guaranteed.

If the ideal amount is not affordable, use a staged plan

One possible progressionStage 1: establish a repeatable minimum saving habitStage 2: build a liquidity bufferStage 3: increase contributions after debt, income or fixed-cost improvementsStage 4: revisit goal timelines if the required amount remains unrealistic

Frequently asked questions

Is 10% enough?

It may be a useful start, or it may be insufficient for a particular goal. The answer depends on the target, timeline, existing assets and other obligations.

Should I save before paying off debt?

That depends on debt cost, emergency liquidity and the consequences of missing payments. High-cost debt and basic cash resilience often deserve attention alongside long-term goals.

Should I increase savings after a raise?

A raise is a natural review point because increasing a contribution before the higher income is fully absorbed by spending can improve the savings rate with less disruption.
