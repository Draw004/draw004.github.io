RETIREMENT INCOME GUIDE

How much monthly income will you need in retirement?

Translate the lifestyle you expect to fund into a monthly retirement spending estimate before solving for the portfolio behind it.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Estimate your retirement income needs→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

Retirement monthly income should be estimated from the lifestyle and obligations you expect after work, not by blindly replacing a fixed percentage of salary. Build the budget category by category, subtract dependable income sources, and distinguish essential expenses from spending you could reduce during a difficult market.

BEFORE YOU START

Start with the spending that will still exist after work ends.

Retirement income needs are usually easier to understand as a monthly budget than as one large portfolio number. Begin with the expenses you expect to keep, remove costs that are likely to end, and add costs that may rise or begin later.

Do not assume every expense moves in the same direction.

Commuting or mortgage costs may fall. Travel may rise in early retirement. Healthcare can become more important later. The useful question is not simply what you spend today, but which parts of that spending will still need funding.

Subtract dependable retirement income separately.

If you expect pension income or another dependable cash flow, model it separately from portfolio withdrawals. That shows the amount your savings may actually need to fund rather than treating the whole lifestyle as an investment withdrawal.

Inflation connects today's budget with the future budget.

A monthly budget stated in today's currency should be grown to the retirement date before comparing it with future income and assets.

Start with spending, not income replacement

Rules such as “replace 70% or 80% of salary” can be useful rough checks, but they hide how households differ. Payroll taxes, commuting, mortgages and child-related costs may fall; travel, leisure, healthcare or care costs may rise. A bottom-up retirement budget is more transparent.

Separate essential, flexible and one-time spending

This classification matters because flexible spending is a risk-management tool. A withdrawal plan where 40% of spending can be deferred has more room to respond to a market decline than a plan where nearly every rupee is essential.

Subtract income sources by timing and reliability

Map each pension, benefit, annuity, rental income stream or planned work income onto the retirement timeline. Some begin later, some are fixed in nominal terms, and some are uncertain. The portfolio needs to bridge gaps rather than simply fund the same percentage every year.

Build an irregular-expense reserve

Monthly averages can hide car replacement, home repairs, dental work, insurance premiums and family events. Add an annual or multi-year sinking-fund line so these costs do not appear as “surprises” that force an investment sale at the wrong time.

Inflate categories differently when the distinction matters

A single inflation rate is simple, but healthcare, rent, education support, travel and energy can move differently from the broad consumer-price index. For long retirements, testing one or two category-specific stress rates can reveal where the budget is most fragile.

Turn the budget into a retirement-income range

  1. Calculate a floor for essential spending.
  2. Calculate a base budget for the lifestyle you reasonably expect.
  3. Calculate a high scenario with travel, gifts, healthcare or other optional/uncertain spending.
  4. Model each against pensions and the portfolio so you know which layer could be adjusted if conditions change.

Create an income ladder that matches spending certainty

Essential expenses benefit from dependable cash flow. Flexible expenses can tolerate more market dependence. A retirement-income design can therefore map pensions or other stable income to the spending floor, maintain liquid assets for near-term withdrawals, and let diversified growth assets support later/flexible spending. The exact instruments differ by country, but the liability-matching logic is universal.

Model survivor income for couples

After one partner dies, some pensions or benefits may shrink while housing, utilities and many fixed costs do not fall by half. A joint retirement budget should therefore include a survivor scenario, not simply divide all household spending by two.

Gross income can hide taxes

Two portfolios producing the same gross withdrawal can create different after-tax cash flow depending on account type and jurisdiction. Keep the lifestyle budget after-tax and then estimate the gross withdrawal needed to fund it, rather than assuming every rupee leaving the account is spendable.

Frequently asked questions

Should retirement income equal a percentage of final salary?

Use salary replacement only as a rough check. A category-by-category budget is more useful because work-related costs, taxes, housing and leisure can change materially.

Should I include taxes in the monthly number?

Yes. Decide whether your spending target is before or after tax, then model withdrawals and income consistently. Tax rules differ by country and account type.

How do I handle large expenses that are not monthly?

Convert predictable irregular expenses into an annual or monthly reserve, or model them explicitly in the year they occur.

Why separate essential from discretionary spending?

Because flexible spending can be adjusted when markets or inflation are unfavorable, which can reduce pressure on a portfolio during vulnerable periods.
