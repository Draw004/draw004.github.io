INFLATION GUIDE

How much will today’s monthly expenses cost in 10, 20 and 30 years?

A current monthly budget can become a much larger nominal amount over a long horizon even when the lifestyle itself does not change.

CarrowmontUpdated 28 September 2026Educational planning guide

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

Future cost is the nominal amount a goal may require after prices compound over time. It depends on today’s price, the number of years and the inflation rate relevant to that specific category. A single broad inflation assumption is convenient, but education, healthcare, rent and construction costs can behave differently.

BEFORE YOU START

Inflation compounds the price level over time.

If a household wants to preserve roughly the same purchasing power, the nominal budget required in the future usually rises when prices rise.

The lifestyle can be unchanged while the currency amount rises.

A larger future number does not automatically mean a more expensive lifestyle in real terms. Part of the increase may simply compensate for lower purchasing power per currency unit.

Different expenses can have different inflation.

Housing, healthcare, education, food and travel may not move together. For important goals, test whether one headline inflation assumption is enough.

Use future costs when solving for future funding.

If a goal is described in today's money, first estimate its future nominal cost, then compare that future target with future savings and investment values.

For a $50,000 goal today, a two-percentage-point difference in inflation becomes increasingly important over long horizons. That is why a goal plan should not hide the inflation assumption inside a single future target.

Use goal-specific inflation when it materially improves the plan

Broad CPI can be reasonable for general lifestyle spending. For a goal dominated by one category—tuition, medical care, rent, construction or travel—a separate stress assumption can be more informative. Avoid false precision: the objective is to see sensitivity, not claim that a category will rise at exactly one rate.

Do not inflate costs that are already quoted in future money

If a university provides an expected fee for the year your child will attend, or a contract specifies a future payment, adding general inflation again can double-count the increase. First identify whether each input is in today’s money or future nominal money.

Irregular goals need a payment schedule, not one date

Education may require payments over four years; a home renovation may occur in phases; retirement spending happens every month. Inflate each cash flow to its own date rather than applying one factor to a total that is paid across several years.

Inflation interacts with investment return

A goal can rise at 7% while a savings portfolio earns 8%, leaving only about 0.93% real growth before tax and fees. The nominal return looks strong, but the gap between asset growth and goal-cost growth is what determines progress.

Goal-specific real growth(1 + portfolio return) ÷ (1 + goal inflation) − 1

A robust goal uses a range

  1. Estimate current cost and time horizon.
  2. Choose a base inflation assumption appropriate to the goal.
  3. Test at least one higher rate.
  4. Project current assets and contributions under realistic net returns.
  5. Recalculate when actual goal prices or timing change.

Sensitivity grows with both time and the inflation gap

A one-percentage-point difference over two years is small; over 25 years it can be enormous. This means the exact inflation assumption matters most for long-dated, high-value goals. For a goal due next year, effort is better spent estimating the current market price accurately.

Scope inflation and price inflation are different

A future wedding, renovation or education goal can become more expensive because the underlying prices rise or because the desired scope grows. Keep those effects separate. Otherwise lifestyle upgrades can be mislabeled as “inflation,” making the forecast hard to manage.

Update from observed prices, not from the old forecast

If a goal cost $50,000 when the plan was created and now actually costs $60,000, use $60,000 as the new base. Do not keep compounding the old estimate just because that was the original spreadsheet. Actual prices contain more information than a stale forecast.

Separate uncertainty in the price from uncertainty in the goal itself

A future-cost estimate can be wrong for two very different reasons. Prices may rise faster or slower than assumed, and the thing you plan to buy may change. A larger home, a different course, more guests or a different travel standard is a scope change—not inflation. Keeping a short note of today’s goal specification alongside the current price makes later reviews more useful because you can tell whether the cost increased because of prices or because the goal became larger.

Use a sensitivity band instead of a single target

For long-dated goals, show the target under a lower, base and higher inflation assumption. If all three cases are comfortably funded, the plan is robust to reasonable inflation error. If the plan works only in the lowest case, the contribution or goal design deserves attention now rather than after years of compounding.

Frequently asked questions

Should every goal use the same inflation rate?

Not necessarily. A general rate is convenient, but category-specific stress assumptions can be useful when one type of cost dominates the goal.

What if I already know the future quoted price?

Treat it as a future nominal cash flow unless the quote is explicitly stated in today’s money. Do not automatically inflate it again.

How often should I update future-cost estimates?

When actual market prices, target date, scope or inflation conditions change materially; an annual review is a practical cadence for long goals.

Why does a small inflation change matter so much?

Because the difference compounds every year. Over 15–30 years, two percentage points can materially change the future target.
