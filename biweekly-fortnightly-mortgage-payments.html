Biweekly or fortnightly mortgage payments: where do the savings actually come from?

Every two weeks and twice monthly sound similar, but they create different annual payment counts and can lead to very different loan outcomes.

By CarrowmontUpdated 11 Oct 2026Educational guideMethodology

Country-aware terminology

Carrowmont keeps the maths universal and adapts familiar mortgage or home-loan wording to the country you select. Lender rules and fees still depend on the actual loan contract.

Direct answer

The key reason a true every-two-weeks plan can accelerate payoff is that 26 half-payments equal 13 full monthly-payment equivalents in a year. Paying twice per month is different: 24 half-payments equal only 12 full payments. The calendar distinction is simple, but lender processing and interest conventions can change the exact savings.

Biweekly, fortnightly and twice monthly are not the same

Every two weeks means 26 payment dates in a normal 52-week year. Two payments per month means 24 payment dates. If each payment equals half the normal monthly amount, every-two-weeks produces one extra full-payment equivalent over the year while twice-monthly does not. This is the mechanical source of much of the advertised payoff benefit.

Illustrative 25-year comparison

Using Carrowmont’s standard fixed-rate illustration, a 1000000 balance at 6% over 25 years has a monthly scheduled payment of about 6443.01. Half of that is 3221.51. Paying that half amount every two weeks results in 26 payments per year, or the cash equivalent of 13 monthly payments.

The exact outcome can differ because a real lender may calculate interest daily, hold partial payments until a full payment is assembled, apply a different compounding convention or charge for a special payment plan.

Frequency alone is not magic

If a lender simply divides the same annual cash into more payment dates, the benefit may be small. The large payoff change usually appears because the every-two-weeks pattern causes more cash to reach principal over the year. That is why consumer guidance often explains the approach in terms of one extra monthly-payment equivalent rather than presenting biweekly timing as a special interest-rate trick.

Country terminology changes, but the arithmetic does not

“Biweekly” is common in the United States and Canada. “Fortnightly” is more familiar in the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand and some other markets. Carrowmont localizes that label, but both refer to an every-14-days cadence with about 26 payment periods per year. Semi-monthly or twice-monthly remains a different 24-period schedule.

Questions to ask before switching repayment frequency

  • Will the lender apply each half-payment immediately, or hold it until a full payment is received?
  • Does the new schedule actually increase total annual principal paid?
  • Is there a setup or service fee for the payment plan?
  • Are there prepayment restrictions or penalties?
  • Does the loan use daily interest, a special compounding convention or another method that changes the precise result?
  • Would simply making one deliberate extra payment per year achieve the same goal with fewer moving parts?

Why a do-it-yourself extra payment may be simpler

The CFPB notes that borrowers may be able to achieve a similar effect without paying for a biweekly service by making one extra monthly mortgage payment each year, provided the loan terms allow it and the money is applied appropriately. The practical advantage is control: the borrower can choose when to make the extra payment instead of signing up for a third-party schedule.

Pay-cycle convenience is separate from lender repayment cadence

A household can be paid every two weeks while the mortgage is contractually due monthly. Those are separate facts. Carrowmont deliberately keeps pay-cycle planning and scheduled loan repayment frequency separate so a user can set aside money per payday without pretending the lender itself collects every two weeks. This distinction prevents a budgeting preference from silently changing amortization.

What happens in a 27-paycheck year?

Some every-two-weeks payroll calendars produce 27 paychecks in certain years. That does not automatically mean the mortgage contract has 27 half-payments or that the extra paycheck should be sent to the lender. It simply creates a cash-flow opportunity. A household could direct some or all of that extra-paycheck capacity to principal, savings or another goal depending on its priorities.

How to model the idea without confusing frequencies

  1. Identify the lender’s actual scheduled repayment frequency.
  2. Identify your pay frequency separately.
  3. If you want a true every-two-weeks loan scenario, choose that scheduled frequency only if it matches the loan.
  4. If the loan remains monthly, model the extra annual principal separately instead of changing the lender cadence.
  5. Compare the total annual cash paid as well as the payoff date.

Frequently asked questions

Are biweekly and fortnightly the same?

Yes in cadence: both mean every two weeks. The preferred word depends on country.

Is twice monthly the same as biweekly?

No. Twice monthly normally means 24 payments a year; every two weeks normally means 26.

Do 26 half-payments always save the same number of years?

No. The effect depends on balance, rate, term, interest convention and how the lender processes the payments.

Can I keep a monthly mortgage and still get the “extra payment” effect?

Often yes, subject to the contract. A separate annual extra principal payment can create a similar increase in annual cash paid without changing the contractual schedule.
