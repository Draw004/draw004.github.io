MONTHLY INVESTMENT GUIDE

How much should you invest each month to reach a financial goal?

Instead of starting with a monthly amount and hoping it is enough, work backward from the goal, time horizon and assumptions.

CarrowmontPublished 23 September 2026 · Reviewed 21 October 2026Editorial standardsEducational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Calculate the monthly investment for your goal→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

30-second answer

The monthly contribution should be worked backward from the goal amount, time available, current money already dedicated to the goal and a cautious range of return assumptions. It is an output of the plan, not a universal percentage of income.

WORK BACKWARD FROM THE GOAL

The monthly amount is an output, not the starting point.

“How much should I invest each month?” cannot be answered responsibly with one percentage for everyone. A useful calculation starts with a defined future goal and then solves for the contribution needed under transparent assumptions.

Start with the goal in future money.

If the goal is stated in today's purchasing power, estimate what it may cost at the target date. For example, something costing $200,000 today would cost about $296,049 after 10 years at 4% annual inflation. That is an illustration, not a forecast of the actual price.

Interpretation: A higher assumed return makes the required contribution look smaller, but it also makes the plan more dependent on an uncertain outcome. Do not solve an affordability problem simply by raising the return assumption.

Time can be a more dependable lever than optimism.

Using the same target and a 6% annual assumption, a shorter horizon requires a larger monthly contribution because there are fewer contributions and fewer compounding periods.

In the worked example above, extending the horizon from 10 to 12 years at 6% reduces the modeled monthly requirement from about $970.40 to about $701.70. A later date is not always possible, but it is a real planning lever rather than an assumed market outcome.

Three levers solve most funding gaps.

LeverWhat changesTrade-off
ContributionIncrease the amount invested each monthUses more current cash flow
TimeMove a flexible goal laterMay not be possible for fixed-date goals
TargetReduce or redesign the goalChanges the outcome being funded
Return assumptionModel a higher expected returnDoes not create a guaranteed return and may imply more risk

Near-term goals may need lower-risk assets even if the monthly amount rises.

A higher-risk investment can make a calculator show a lower required contribution, but a large loss close to the goal date may be more damaging than a higher saving requirement. Match the investment risk to the goal's horizon and flexibility rather than to the size of the target.

Contribution increases should be explicit.

Some people keep contributions fixed. Others plan to increase them as income rises. Any increase assumption should be checked against expected affordability rather than treated as automatic.

Checklist: Calculate a monthly amount you can actually use

  • Is the goal amount stated in today's money or future money?
  • What is the target date, and can it move?
  • How much existing money is genuinely dedicated to this goal?
  • What monthly amount is affordable without weakening emergency liquidity?
  • Have you tested more than one return assumption?
  • Are fees and relevant taxes reflected in the plan?
  • Does the chosen investment risk fit the goal date?
  • When will you review the target, balance and contribution again?

Several goals should usually be modeled separately.

A home deposit in five years and retirement in 30 years have different dates, risk capacity and liquidity needs. Combining them into one “monthly investment” number can hide whether either goal is actually on track.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use the highest return assumption to keep the monthly amount affordable?

No. That can make the plan look easier without changing the uncertainty of future returns. Test contribution, time and target size before depending on a more optimistic market outcome.

What if I already have money saved for the goal?

Include the amount genuinely earmarked for that goal. Its modeled future value reduces the part that future contributions need to fund.

How often should I recalculate?

Review after major changes to the goal, balance, contribution, horizon or investment assumptions. An annual review is a practical cadence for many long-term goals.
