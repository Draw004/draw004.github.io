MONTHLY INVESTMENT GUIDE

How much should you invest each month to reach a financial goal?

Instead of starting with a monthly amount and hoping it is enough, work backward from the goal, time horizon and assumptions.

CarrowmontUpdated 8 October 2026Educational planning guide
WANT TO START WITH YOUR NUMBERS?Calculate the monthly investment for your goal→

Prefer to understand the assumptions first? Start with the basics below, then return to the calculator with more context.

Direct answer

The monthly amount needed for a goal is the contribution that closes the gap between the future target and what your current balance could grow to. It is highly sensitive to time, the target amount and the return assumption, so the result should be treated as a planning requirement under stated assumptions—not a guaranteed prescription.

BEFORE YOU START

The monthly amount is an output of the goal, not the starting point.

A common planning mistake is to pick a comfortable monthly amount first and then assume it will fund the goal. A clearer approach is to define the goal amount, the date, any savings already available and a reasonable range of growth assumptions. Then solve for the monthly contribution.

Illustrative target: 100,000 in 10 yearsAt a 6% annual growth assumption, the required monthly contribution is about 615.At an 8% annual growth assumption, it is about 555.These figures use an equivalent monthly compound rate and end-of-month contributions.

The difference shows why return assumptions affect the answer. It also shows why a lower monthly figure is not necessarily "better" if it depends on a more optimistic assumption.

Five inputs usually drive the result

Target amount. What do you actually want available at the goal date?

Time horizon. More time generally lowers the monthly amount because there are more contributions and more time for growth.

Existing savings. Money already set aside for the goal can reduce the amount that new contributions need to fund.

Growth assumption. A higher assumed return lowers the calculated contribution but increases dependence on investment performance.

Inflation. If the goal is stated in today's money, it may need to be grown to a future nominal amount first.

Why time is often the easiest variable to underestimate

Using the same 100,000 target and a 6% annual assumption, a five-year horizon requires roughly 1,439 per month, a 10-year horizon about 615, and a 15-year horizon about 349. The target did not change. The time available did.

Planning insight: if the required monthly amount feels unrealistic, the useful response is not automatically to assume a higher return. You can also revisit the goal amount, deadline, existing resources or contribution path.

Should contributions stay fixed forever?

Not necessarily. Some people prefer a fixed contribution. Others plan to increase the amount as income rises. A step-up contribution can materially change the ending value, but it also means future monthly commitments become larger. Any step-up assumption should therefore be checked against expected affordability.

What if you have several goals?

Separate them. A home deposit in five years and retirement in 30 years have different time horizons, risk capacity and liquidity needs. Combining every goal into one account can make it hard to see whether each target is actually funded.

Start by defining the goal in future money

A goal such as education, a home deposit or a major purchase often becomes more expensive over time. Estimate the future cost first. Otherwise you may calculate the contribution needed to reach today’s price rather than the amount likely required when the goal arrives.

Goal funding equationTarget = Existing balance growth + Future value of monthly contributions

Illustrative $250,000 goal in 10 yearsAt 6%: about $1,525.5 per month if starting from zero.At 8%: about $1,366.5 per month.At 10%: about $1,220.4 per month.A plan that works only at the highest assumption is more fragile than one that works across a range.

Match risk to the goal date, not to the size of the target

A very large goal does not automatically justify taking more investment risk. If the date is near and fixed, a large loss can be more damaging because there is little time to recover. Long-dated goals may have more capacity for volatility, but the investor’s tolerance and the goal’s flexibility still matter.

Build a contribution path instead of one permanent number

A monthly requirement calculated today does not have to remain fixed for 15 years. You can model planned increases after salary growth, temporary pauses, or a lump sum. The important point is to make those assumptions explicit so the plan does not silently depend on future behaviour that has never been tested.

Review funded status, not only the monthly number

  • Recalculate when the goal cost changes materially.
  • Track current assets that are truly allocated to the goal.
  • Compare current funded percentage with the remaining time.
  • Use lower-return scenarios to see how much contribution flexibility you may need.
  • As the goal nears, reconsider the level of market risk appropriate for money that will soon be spent.

A monthly contribution is really a negotiated trade-off among goal, time and current life

Solving for PMT gives a mathematically required amount, but the household must decide whether that amount is affordable without creating fragility elsewhere. A good plan protects emergency liquidity and essential insurance before directing every spare unit of cash to a distant goal.

Use a funding hierarchy when several goals compete

A near-term mandatory payment, a medium-term home goal and a 30-year retirement goal should not automatically receive proportional shares. Rank each by importance, time horizon and flexibility. This prevents the monthly answer for one calculator from ignoring the rest of the household balance sheet.

Contribution increases are usually safer than return increases

If the target is underfunded, raising the assumed return can make the spreadsheet work without changing reality. Increasing the contribution, extending a flexible date, or reducing the target changes an actual decision variable. Model return uncertainty, but do not use an optimistic assumption as the primary repair.

  • Check whether the target is in future money.
  • Protect short-term liquidity before locking in a contribution.
  • Review at least annually and after major life changes.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use the highest return assumption to keep the monthly amount affordable?

No. That can make the plan look easier without changing the uncertainty of future returns. If the calculated amount is unaffordable, test time, target size and a step-up schedule explicitly.

What if I already have money saved for the goal?

Include only the amount genuinely earmarked for that goal. The existing balance can compound and reduce the required future contribution.

How often should I recalculate?

At least after major changes to the goal, contribution, starting balance or time horizon. An annual review is a practical cadence for many long-term goals.
