Investment fees and expense ratios: How much do they matter?

Investment returns are uncertain, but fees are a cost you can inspect. A small annual percentage can matter because it reduces the amount left in the portfolio to compound.

By CarrowmontPublished 25 November 2026 · Reviewed 25 November 2026Educational guideEditorial Policy

Compare the same contribution plan using a gross return assumption and a lower net return after fees.

30-second answer

Investment fees reduce the return that stays in your portfolio. Some are paid when you buy or sell; others are deducted every year from a fund, account or advisory relationship. An expense ratio is an annual operating cost expressed as a percentage of fund assets. A difference that looks small each year can become meaningful over a long horizon because the lost money also loses future compounding.

Fee names, disclosure documents, taxes and distribution structures vary by market. Compare the total cost of owning and using an investment under your local disclosure rules, not just one headline fee.

Why fees deserve a separate decision

Investment returns are not promised. Fees are much more observable. If two investments provide the same gross return before costs, the lower-cost option leaves more of that return with the investor. That does not mean the cheapest product is automatically best—strategy, tracking, service, tax and risk also matter—but cost is one variable you do not need to guess.

The main types of investment cost

Bid-ask spreadDifference between buying and selling pricesActs like a trading friction even when commission is zero

What an expense ratio actually means

If a fund has a 0.50% annual expense ratio, the operating cost is roughly 0.50% of the assets each year, subject to the fund's actual accounting and disclosure method. You usually do not receive a separate bill: The cost is reflected in the fund's net performance.

A 0.50% fee does not mean the fund will underperform another fund by exactly 0.50% every year because the underlying holdings and tracking can differ. But, all else equal, higher ongoing costs create a higher performance hurdle.

The compounding effect of a small annual fee

Consider 100,000 growing for 20 years. If the gross return were 7% every year, the ending value before fees would be about 386,968. At a 6.75% net return, it would be about 369,000; at 6% net, about 321,000. These are simplified illustrations, not forecasts. The point is that the annual difference compounds because each year's fee also removes money that could have generated future returns.

Zero commission does not mean zero cost

A platform can offer zero trading commission while the investment still has fund expenses, spreads, currency-conversion costs, account charges or other fees. Always ask what the total cost is to buy, hold and sell.

Compare cost in the context of the job being done

A higher fee can sometimes pay for a service you genuinely value: Financial planning, tax administration, specialist management or access to a structure that solves a specific need. The question is not “Is any fee bad?” It is “What am I receiving, what is the all-in cost, and is there a lower-cost way to achieve the same objective?”

Fees can interact with portfolio behavior

Frequent switching can add transaction costs and can trigger taxes in some jurisdictions. A low-cost fund used in a high-turnover strategy can still become expensive. Conversely, a simple long-term portfolio may keep both explicit and behavioral costs lower.

A practical cost checklist

  1. Identify every one-off cost to buy or sell.
  2. Find the annual fund expense or ongoing charge.
  3. Add account, platform and advisory fees.
  4. Check currency-conversion and spread costs if relevant.
  5. Ask whether any early-exit or redemption charge applies.
  6. Compare the expected service and investment exposure, not just the fee percentage.
  7. Model the fee difference over the full time horizon.

Common mistakes

  • Looking only at commission: Ongoing product costs may be larger.
  • Comparing fees without comparing exposure: Two funds can charge different amounts because they do different jobs.
  • Ignoring small annual percentages: Long horizons magnify recurring differences.
  • Assuming high fees guarantee better performance: A higher cost creates a higher hurdle; it does not create a higher return.
  • Forgetting taxes and spreads: Total investor cost can be wider than the published fund expense.

Frequently asked questions

Is the lowest-fee fund always best?

No. Compare the investment objective, holdings, tracking, risk, liquidity, tax treatment and service. Cost matters, but it is one part of suitability.

What is a good expense ratio?

There is no universal number because products and markets differ. Compare like with like and ask whether the fee is competitive for the exposure and service provided.

Do ETFs have fees even with zero commission?

Yes. An ETF can still have an annual expense ratio, bid-ask spread and other trading or account costs.

How should I model fees in a calculator?

A simple planning method is to reduce the gross return assumption by the ongoing annual fee, while remembering that real products may have additional one-off costs and taxes.
