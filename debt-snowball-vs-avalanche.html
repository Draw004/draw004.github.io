Debt snowball vs avalanche: which debt gets paid first?

Both methods use the money you already plan to put toward debt. The difference is simply which debt receives the extra payment first: the smallest balance or the highest interest rate.

By CarrowmontUpdated 14 Oct 2026Educational guideMethodology

Direct answer

Snowball means paying the smallest debt first. Avalanche means paying the debt with the highest interest rate first. When one debt is cleared, its payment is rolled into the next debt instead of disappearing from the plan.

The two methods in plain language

MethodWhat gets the extra payment first?What usually changes?
Smallest debt first (Snowball)The active debt with the smallest remaining balance.The first debt may disappear sooner, which can make progress easier to see.
Highest-interest debt first (Avalanche)The active debt with the highest interest rate.Total interest is often lower when the rate difference is meaningful.

Why the same monthly payment can produce different results

Imagine you have two debts and can pay 700 per month in total. Both methods still use 700. They simply send the amount above the required minimums to a different debt first. That changes how long expensive balances remain outstanding and which account reaches zero first.

There is no need to compare a Snowball plan using one monthly payment with an Avalanche plan using another. A fair comparison keeps the debts and total payment the same.

What happens when one debt is cleared?

The payment that used to go to that debt stays in the plan. It becomes available for the next debt. This is the key idea behind both methods: the monthly debt budget is rolled forward rather than shrinking each time a balance reaches zero.

For example, suppose one debt has a 150 minimum payment and you are also sending 200 of extra money to the current target. If that debt is cleared, the next target can receive the 150 that has been freed as well as the 200 extra amount, on top of its own minimum payment. The total amount you planned to pay toward debt does not need to fall just because one account is finished.

What should stay the same when you compare the methods?

Keep the starting balances, interest rates, minimum payments and total monthly debt payment the same. Only the payoff order should change. This matters because it would be misleading to compare one method using a larger monthly payment with another method using a smaller one. If you want to test a higher payment, apply that same amount to both methods and compare again.

It also helps to avoid changing several assumptions at once. First compare the two payoff orders using your current plan. Then try adding an extra monthly amount. That makes it easier to see whether the bigger difference comes from the payoff order or simply from paying more each month.

If you add 200 per month, the modeled monthly debt budget is 850. Highest-interest-first sends the extra money toward the 20% card. Smallest-debt-first sends it toward whichever active balance is smallest. Once a debt is cleared, that freed payment is added to the next target.

Does Avalanche always save interest?

Highest-interest-first is designed to attack the most expensive rate first, so it often uses less interest in a fixed-rate model. But the difference can be tiny when rates are similar, and sometimes both methods produce the same order. A useful calculator should show the actual difference rather than simply label one method the winner.

Why some people still prefer the smallest debt first

Paying off one small balance quickly can create a visible milestone. The value of that experience is personal and cannot be calculated from interest alone. Carrowmont therefore shows both the first-debt payoff timing and the total interest rather than turning the comparison into a recommendation.

A first payoff can also make the plan easier to manage because there is one fewer account to track. That does not automatically make the smallest-debt method cheaper. It simply means the practical benefit a person values may be different from the mathematical benefit of reducing interest. Looking at both results side by side makes that trade-off visible without pretending there is one correct choice for everyone.

What if the interest-rate difference is small?

If several debts have very similar rates, the interest difference between the two methods may be modest. In that situation, the payoff order can matter less than the amount you consistently pay each month. The calculator still runs both paths because even a small difference is useful to see, but the result should be read in proportion. Saving a small amount of modeled interest may or may not matter more to you than clearing an account sooner.

If the rates are very different, the highest-interest method can create a larger interest advantage because expensive balances are reduced earlier. The calculator should show the actual modeled difference rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

What can make real lender figures differ?

  • Some lenders calculate interest daily rather than monthly.
  • Minimum payments can change as balances fall.
  • Fees, new purchases, penalties or promotional rates can alter the path.
  • Variable interest rates can change after the plan starts.
  • Rounding and statement dates can shift the final payment slightly.

A payoff calculator is a planning model, not a lender settlement quote.

Where an extra monthly payment fits

An extra payment can shorten either plan because more principal is removed earlier. A useful comparison should rerun the same debts with no extra amount so you can see the modeled months and interest saved by the extra payment, rather than only showing a faster final date.

The extra amount should be money you deliberately choose to put toward debt after considering the rest of your household plan. Carrowmont does not assume that every amount left in a budget should automatically go to debt. If you come from the Budget & Cash Flow Planner, the amount is offered for review and must be applied by you.

What should you check before relying on the result?

Compare the calculator inputs with your latest statements. Check the balance, annual interest rate and required minimum payment for every account. If a lender uses a temporary promotional rate, a changing minimum-payment formula, daily interest, fees or a rate that can reset, treat the result as an estimate rather than an exact settlement schedule.

Also check whether making extra payments has any special rule or charge. Most ordinary revolving debts allow extra payments, but loan terms can differ. The calculator does not invent lender rules or penalties. If your agreement contains a charge, restriction or unusual payment allocation rule, use the lender's documents as the source of truth.

How to read the comparison without overthinking it

Start with three numbers: when the first debt is cleared, when all modeled debts are cleared, and total modeled interest. If one method clears the first debt much sooner but costs slightly more interest, that is a real trade-off you can see. If the highest-interest method saves a meaningful amount of interest with almost the same first-payoff timing, that is also clear. The purpose of the comparison is to make those differences visible in plain numbers.

Then look at the extra-payment comparison. If adding a realistic monthly amount changes the debt-free date far more than switching between Snowball and Avalanche, the size of the monthly payment may be the more important planning lever. If the two methods are almost identical, you do not need to force a complicated decision.

Frequently asked questions

What if I have only one debt?

Both methods are identical because there is no payoff-order decision to make.

What if two debts have the same interest rate?

A calculator needs a clear tie rule. Carrowmont uses the lower current balance first for the highest-interest method when rates tie.

What if two debts have the same balance?

For the smallest-debt method, Carrowmont uses the higher interest rate first when balances tie.

Should I stop emergency savings to pay debt faster?

That is a broader household decision. This calculator only models the payment amount you choose. Use the Budget & Cash Flow Planner to review whether the payment fits the rest of your plan.
