Stocks vs bonds: what is the difference?

Buying a stock means owning part of a business; buying a bond generally means lending money to an issuer under stated terms.

Direct answer

Stocks and bonds make different claims on an issuer. Stockholders own residual equity in a business; bondholders lend money under contractual terms. Stocks generally have greater upside and greater uncertainty, while bonds can provide more predictable cash flows but remain exposed to interest-rate, credit, inflation and liquidity risks.

Stocks represent ownership; bonds represent debt.

A shareholder participates in the fortunes of a company through changes in its value and possible dividends. A bond investor lends to a government, company or other issuer and may receive contractual interest plus repayment of principal if the issuer meets its obligations.

Interest rates matter to bond prices.

Investor.gov explains that market interest rates and prices of existing fixed-rate bonds generally move in opposite directions. If new bonds offer higher rates, an older lower-coupon bond may need to fall in price to compete.

“Bonds are safe” is too broad.

Government bonds, investment-grade corporate bonds and high-yield bonds can have very different credit and price risk. Longer maturities can also be more sensitive to interest-rate changes. The label “bond” does not describe one uniform risk level.

Bond prices can fall even when the issuer keeps paying

Fixed-rate bond prices generally move inversely to market interest rates. If new bonds offer higher yields, an older low-coupon bond becomes less attractive and may trade below face value. Holding to maturity can reduce the relevance of interim price moves if the issuer pays as promised, but liquidity needs can force a sale earlier.

Duration measures interest-rate sensitivity

Longer-maturity, lower-coupon bonds usually have greater price sensitivity to rate changes. A bond fund does not “mature” in the same way as one individual bond because the fund continuously holds a portfolio. Investors should understand the fund’s duration and credit exposure rather than assuming all fixed income is low risk.

Inflation attacks fixed nominal payments

A bond can pay every promised coupon and still disappoint in real terms if inflation is high. Inflation-linked bonds attempt to address this through payments or principal linked to an inflation index, but product mechanics and tax treatment differ by country.

Why portfolios often hold both

Stocks can provide long-term growth; high-quality bonds can provide income, liquidity and a different return pattern. The correlation is not permanently negative—there are periods when stocks and bonds fall together—so diversification should be stress-tested rather than assumed.

Do not compare yields without credit risk

A high-yield bond may pay more because the market demands compensation for a greater probability of default or loss. The extra yield is not free income. Compare credit quality, maturity, seniority and liquidity, not only the coupon.

Bond “safety” depends on duration and credit, not the word bond

A short-term high-quality government bond and a 30-year low-quality corporate bond have very different risk. Duration approximates sensitivity to interest-rate changes; credit quality reflects the risk that promised payments are not made. A portfolio can be conservative in one dimension and aggressive in the other.

Yield to maturity is not a guaranteed realized return

For an individual bond, yield to maturity assumes coupons are reinvested at the same yield and the issuer pays as promised. Selling before maturity introduces market-price risk. For a bond fund, holdings roll over continuously, so there is no single maturity date that returns the original principal.

Stocks and bonds can fall together

A rapid inflation/rate shock can reduce bond prices while also compressing equity valuations. The diversification relationship changes over time. Hold bonds because their cash-flow and risk properties fit the plan, not because correlation with stocks is assumed to be permanently negative.

Frequently asked questions

Are bonds always safer than stocks?

No. High-quality short-duration bonds may be less volatile, but low-quality, long-duration or illiquid bonds can carry substantial risk.

Why do bond prices fall when rates rise?

New bonds offer higher yields, so existing lower-coupon bonds must usually trade at a discount to compete.

Can stocks and bonds both fall at the same time?

Yes. Inflation shocks and rapidly rising rates can pressure both equity valuations and bond prices.

Does a bond fund return principal at maturity?

A bond fund usually holds many bonds with rolling maturities and has no single maturity date. Its price and income depend on the portfolio over time.
