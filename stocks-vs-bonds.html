Stocks vs bonds: what is the difference?

Buying a stock means owning part of a business; buying a bond generally means lending money to an issuer under stated terms.

CarrowmontUpdated 27 September 2026Educational planning guide

Stocks represent ownership; bonds represent debt.

A shareholder participates in the fortunes of a company through changes in its value and possible dividends. A bond investor lends to a government, company or other issuer and may receive contractual interest plus repayment of principal if the issuer meets its obligations.

DimensionStocksBonds
Economic claimOwnership in a companyLoan to an issuer
Potential incomeDividends may be paid but are not guaranteedCoupon/interest may be contractual depending on the bond
Price volatilityCan be highOften lower than stocks, but varies widely by bond type and maturity
Key risksBusiness, market, valuation and dilution riskInterest-rate, credit/default, inflation and liquidity risk
UpsidePotentially large if businesses grow stronglyUsually more limited by contractual payments for plain fixed-rate bonds

Interest rates matter to bond prices.

Investor.gov explains that market interest rates and prices of existing fixed-rate bonds generally move in opposite directions. If new bonds offer higher rates, an older lower-coupon bond may need to fall in price to compete.

“Bonds are safe” is too broad.

Government bonds, investment-grade corporate bonds and high-yield bonds can have very different credit and price risk. Longer maturities can also be more sensitive to interest-rate changes. The label “bond” does not describe one uniform risk level.

Frequently asked questions

Do bonds always rise when stocks fall?

No. Correlations change, and both can decline in the same period, especially when inflation or interest-rate shocks affect valuations.

Why hold bonds if stocks may grow more?

Bonds can provide income, diversification and lower volatility in some portfolios. The appropriate role depends on time horizon, spending needs and risk capacity.

Can I lose money on a bond?

Yes. An issuer can default, market rates can move against the bond, liquidity can dry up, or inflation can reduce the real value of fixed payments.
