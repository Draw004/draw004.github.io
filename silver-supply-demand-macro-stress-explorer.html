    METHODOLOGY

    Two engines stay visible instead of becoming a false “silver score”

    The Physical Market Engine adjusts published completed-year supply and demand categories. The Macro Engine separately describes real-yield and U.S.-dollar conditions. The combined result uses a transparent rule matrix and remains descriptive.

    Physical modelModeled balance = modeled supply − modeled demandNegative = modeled deficit. Positive = modeled surplus. Classification uses balance as a percentage of modeled demand.
    V1 simple sensitivitiesIndustrial ±5% · Physical investment ±10% · Mine supply ±2% · Recycling ±10%These are scenario step sizes, not forecasts.

    Inventory limitation

    A flow deficit can contribute to physical tightness, but above-ground bullion, exchange inventories, investor holdings and fabrication inventories can release metal. V1 therefore never translates a deficit into “silver is running out.”

    Source rounding

    The source categories are published to one decimal place. Their displayed category sum can differ slightly from the published headline total. Carrowmont preserves the published total and balance separately and discloses the rounding difference.

    WHY SILVER IS DIFFERENT FROM GOLD

    Two precious metals, different demand structures

    Gold

    • Primarily monetary, investment and reserve demand.
    • Lower direct industrial exposure relative to silver.
    • Often treated as a safe-haven and reserve-diversification asset.

    Explore gold’s macro forces →

    Silver

    • Precious and investment metal plus a major industrial input.
    • More exposed to fabrication cycles and technology demand.
    • Mine and recycling responses can differ from gold and silver is generally more volatile.

    UNDERSTAND THE OFFSETS

    Demand growth can trigger responses elsewhere

    Industrial demand

    Electronics, photovoltaics, vehicles, grid infrastructure and other uses make industrial fabrication the largest demand category in the reference year.

    Physical investment

    Coin and net-bar demand can shift quickly with investor sentiment and macro uncertainty, making it an important but volatile demand center.

    Mine supply

    Much silver is produced as a by-product of lead-zinc, copper and gold mining, so supply may not respond quickly to silver prices alone.

    Recycling

    Higher prices and available scrap can encourage more recycling, but collection, refining capacity and the composition of scrap create lags.

    Thrifting and substitution

    Manufacturers can reduce silver loadings or substitute materials where technically and economically viable. Photovoltaics provide a current example of this counterforce.

    Real yields and the dollar

    Silver can still face precious-metal headwinds when real yields or the U.S. dollar are strong even if the physical flow remains in deficit.

    FAQ

    Common questions

    Does a deficit guarantee a higher silver price?

    No. A deficit can contribute to tightness, but prices also respond to inventories, investment flows, monetary conditions, recycling, substitution and broader economic conditions.

    Why use completed-year data rather than the latest forecast?

    The default engine starts from the latest completed full year so a forecast is not silently presented as history. Current-year outlooks can be discussed as context without replacing the reference base.

    Why does Carrowmont keep physical and macro results separate?

    Silver can have a tight physical market while facing high real yields or a strong dollar. Keeping both engines visible prevents a single score from hiding that conflict.

    Does changing country or currency alter the global silver quantities?

    No. The physical market is global. Ounces and tonnes are not converted into a local currency, and country selection elsewhere on Carrowmont does not change the global reference.

    Is this investment advice?

    No. It is educational scenario analysis and does not recommend buying, selling, holding or allocating to silver.
